## shAkta general
Over the past several thousand years, the veneration of the Great Goddess has also taken many forms. In this tradition we include types of Śākta traditions that do not associate themselves with the classical Śākta Āgamas but also do not affiliate with the early modern East Indian goddess traditions, such as the worship of Tārā, Dakṣinakālī, or the Mahāvidyās. 

We also include here works that are primarily focused on alchemy, defensive and practical magic, and the types of transactional tantric practice that for many today represent the most familiar face of “Mantra-Tantra.” The occasional worship of Durgā and various forms of Purāṇic goddess worship are also treated as representing the general (sāmānya) form of Śākta tradition.

## kaulism
### Ritual focus
Kaulism is perhaps best understood as a style of worship as much as it is a separate tradition, and as such Kaula modes for venerating a range of deities have been integrated into many, if not most, of the tantras in the MIRI archive. The Kaula traditions seem to originate in the veneration of generic gods and goddesses of the Cakra (Cakreśvara / Cakreśvarī), paired with the Eight Mother Goddesses, with worship using transgressive substances (originally just three, as opposed to the Pañcamākaras) conducted in a style derived from the Kāpālikas. 

Conceptually, these early Kaula traditions tend to emphasize the centrality of the human body as a primary site for ritual and ecstatic experience, even to the extent of treating spiritual experience (anubhava) as a veridical authority (pramāṇa). 

### Non dualism
The tradition that descends from the Kulasāra, one of the earliest of these works, teaches a theology of radical non-dualism, distinct from Advaita Vedānta, that it calls Paramādvaita. The same work is also the first to explicitly present the role of the Jīvanmukta, who “liberated, causes others to be liberated.” Both frameworks would prove pivotal to the non-dual Śaivism of Kashmir.

### Phases
Kaula textuality can be divided into several phases. 

The first major distinction is between early texts that speak generically about embodied incarnation and those works (for example, the Kaulajñānanirṇaya or the Akulavīra tantras) that feature the siddha Matsyendranātha and his disciples as primary revealers of Kaula traditions in the current age. 

In a later phase of these discourses, the Kulārṇavatantra, with its equation of Kaulism and the authority of the Upaniṣads, emerges as a central locus of scriptural authority. 

The tradition also includes a number of early modern works that frame their Kaulism around conceptual categories drawn from East Indian tantric traditions without focusing on the preferred deities of those lineages.

## Northeast Indian Tantra
This tradition incorporates a distinct corpus of early modern works of Śākta tantra, ritual manuals, and commentaries that are composed in Mithila, Assam, and Bengal, as well as works that derive the structure of their pantheons from such tantras. The reason the tradition is treated separately is that, with few exceptions, these works represent a conscious break from the structures and pantheons that define the classical Śākta tantras and offer an even more fundamental erasure of the idiom and influence of the Non-dual Śākta tantras of Kashmir. Thus, for example, the Kālīs of the Krama and their scriptural models are replaced by a different sequence of sixteen Kālī-Nityā goddesses, or perhaps a new centrality is given to Dakṣiṇākālī standing upon the body of Śiva.

We also see a new focus on figures such as Tārā and Ugratārā in her many manifestations, or Nīlasarasvatī. In place of the Āmnāya system, emerging out of Mithila around the sixteenth century (they are known, for example, to Āgamavāgīśa in his Bṛhattantrasāra) and then spreading across the subcontinent, a canon of Ten Mahāvidyā goddesses (often Kālī, Tārā, Tripurasundarī, Bhuvaneśvarī, Bhairavī, Chinnamastā, Dhūmavatī, Bagalāmukhī, Mataṅgī, and Kamalā) becomes increasingly popular. These deities are then treated both as a set of resources for applied mantra-prayogas and as the core pantheon whose mantras must be acquired to gain access to higher levels of practice. 

In the more Kaula sources, frameworks such as dividing modes of practice into those for Paśus, Vīras, and Divyas, each requiring increasing degrees of ritual eligibility and posing greater potential risks, become ubiquitous. In some works affiliated with Tārā, there is the emergence of a new ritual modality, called Mahācīnācāra, said to have been imported from the East. In many cases, there is also an explicit accommodation of Kaula transgressions within a ritual setting to the social standards of an emerging Kulin Brahmanism.

## Bhairava Tantras
In the Classical Tantras, the term “Dakṣiṇa” refers to an eightfold canon of scriptures that take as their supreme deity various forms of the terrifying lord, Bhairava. Though elements drawn from this collection of traditions are preserved in other sources, particularly the Buddhist Kriyātantras and the Jayadrathayāmala, among the foundational texts of these traditions only those concerned with the self-willed lord (Svacchanda Bhairava) and the Lord of Nectar (Amṛtīśa Bhairava) have come down to us intact. 

The Svacchanda Tantra is particularly important, for alone among the early sources it takes as a key focus the teaching of a theology of absolute freedom (svātantrya), a position that is reflected in the social practices of its adherents. 

This tantra also provides us with some of the most detailed accounts of Tantric cosmology and the mechanics of forms of yoga oriented toward observing and manipulating the passage of time and the contemplation of the inner voids. Both this cosmology and some of the textual traditions of tantric yoga seem to be inheritances of the Atimārga tradition. 


In a similar manner, the Netra Tantra of Amṛtīśa Bhairava transmits a series of teachings on apotropaic practices used to defend both a king and a yogī from hostile forces, as well as a threefold series of Śākta Yogas that are closely connected to earlier traditions on cataloguing different types of malevolent beings, the conditions that may render human beings susceptible to their influence, and the corresponding remedies. Though neither text, strictly speaking, adopts such a position, both of these texts were commented on from a non-dual Śaiva perspective by the eleventh-century Kashmiri exegete Kṣemarāja.
