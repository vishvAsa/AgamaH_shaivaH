Śāmbhava
The family of traditions variously labelled Śāmbhava, Ṣaḍanvayaśāmbhava, Śāmbhavī vidyā, and forth, are perhaps the least well understood of the living tantric lineages that claim a textual foundation rooted in classical sources. While this state of affairs is a product of the very preliminary nature of our research, it is also reflects a complex history of multiple receptions across a range of communities. From the perspective of most contemporary Śrīvidyā practitioners, the Ṣaḍanvayaśāmbhava is simply a specialized mode of esoteric practice, much of it associated with the ritual veneration of the Gurupāduka mantras, reserved for elite initiates. In a similar manner, for Kaulas following either in the tradition of the Kulārṇavatantra or working within the Nepalese Āmnāya system, under the guise of the Ūrdhvāmnāya, late stage initiations reveal the presence of the hidden face of the god Śambhu. Often identified with the non-dual teachings of the upaniṣads, Śambhu presides over the totality of the Śākta systems. Within the vernacular traditions of Telugu, Kannada, and Marathi speaking wandering yogīs and householder practitioners, the tradition of Śambhu or Śāmbhavī vidyā is perceived not as a supplementary mode of tantric ritual, but rather, as a yogic transmission comprising a range of meditative practices, often associated with the use of an internal or external consort. Finally, when it has been noticed at all, western scholars have equated the Śāmbhava with the Ṣaḍanvayaśāmbhava, presenting the practice as a lesser know South-Indian tradition. Such scholarship asserts that the Śāmbhavāmnāya is simply a specialized expression of the Paścimāmnāya. In it, the goddess Kubjikā has been decentered in favor of her male consort Naveśvara or Navātman Bhairava. Over time, it is assumed, this minor tradition was mostly absorbed into Śrividyā.

As we begin to explore the Śāmbhava as a textual tradition based on a growing collection of unpublished sources, a more complicated picture has begun to emerge. Core elements of the tradition seem to originate outside the realm of the āmnāya transmissions of Śākta tantra. What early works such as the Śambhunirṇaya (which does not label itself Kaula or incorporate the basic Kaula goddesses) and the Matsyendrasaṃhitā (a Kaula work which privileges this pantheon) share is a common concern with the transmission of an archaic body of yogic knowledge only distantly related to what is taught in classical tantra. From the perspective of these early sources, what we perceive as the heritage of the āmnāya system is treated as a supplementary ritual modality. Unexpectedly, although present from the very beginning, proto-Śrividyā in particular plays a very minor role in these early traditions, a feature that persists in some of the earliest paddhatis. The transformation of the Śāmbhava into the Ṣaḍanvayaśāmbhava appears to occur at the level of commentary, gradually emerging almost contemporaneously in South India and the Himalayas within traditions of exegesis and ritual systemization. In South India, this takes place within the lineage of the South Indian Śivānandanātha, while in Nepal, it occurs under the guidance of Vimalaprabodha. In both cases, teachers who were primarily remembered as Śākta initiates deemphasized the extra-ritualistic yogic dimensions of the Śāmbhava practice and assimilated it more comprehensively into the Āmnāya traditions. 

Further research drawing upon key works such as the Śāktakramanirṇaya of Umākānta should help us to gain a better understanding of the broader history of these developments which seem to be closely related to the practice of of Āmnāyasamaṣṭi in early modern Śākta tantra along with origins of the Ṣaḍāmnāya/Sarvāmnāya.
