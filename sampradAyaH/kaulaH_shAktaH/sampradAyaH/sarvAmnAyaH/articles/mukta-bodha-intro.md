In the eleventh century, within works such as the Ciñciṇīmatasārasamuccaya, the various transmissions of the Classical Śākta tantras—especially the Trika, the Krama, and the Kubjikā traditions—begin to develop a new way of thinking about their relationship with each other. They emerge not as rivals, but as complementary. Nonetheless, they remain distinct parts of a dynamic and unified Classical Śākta tradition, organized in terms of three or more streams corresponding to the cardinal directions. 

When this emergent model of scriptural currents is transferred into the Kathmandu Valley (Bhaktapur, Patan, and Kathmandu) under the Malla kings, it largely displaces other rival approaches to thinking about the tantras and gradually becomes the dominant model for understanding and preserving the Classical Śākta traditions. 

Instrumental to these developments is the thirteenth-century South Indian Śākta yogī Vimalaprabodha, who brings to the court of King Arimalla both his vast library of tantric works and his considerable genius as a ritualist. Indeed, a close reading of his Paścimārcanapaddhati, which despite the title is in fact dedicated to Śambhu, the deity of the Śāmbhavāmnāya, reveals him already to be teaching his own idiosyncratic fusion of Kālīkrama, Śāmbhava, and Kubjikā traditions, within which Lalitā Tripurasundarī plays a subordinate role.

In the realm of scripture, in works such as the Parātantra, for example, a more explicit model of three or four Āmnāyas is then expanded and refined to incorporate an even wider range of traditions. Thus, the Parātantra presents the Adhāmnāya as incorporating the pantheons of Vajrayāna Buddhism, and also places a new emphasis on the significance of the previously more marginal tradition of Śrīvidyā. 

Some of the Parātantra’s solutions, such as preserving a distinct Dakṣiṇāmnāya for the Bhairava tradition while treating Śrīvidyā as the Ūrdhvāmnāya, would eventually be substituted for treating Śrīvidyā itself as the Dakṣiṇa stream while inserting the increasingly popular Kaula tradition of the Kulārṇava and sometimes also the Śāmbhava at the apex. 

Nevertheless, the general structure of this Sarvāmnāya—“All the Traditions”—approach, with the Trika as the Pūrva, the Krama as the Uttara, the Kubjikā as the Paścima, and Śrīvidyā as the Dakṣiṇa, becomes profoundly important for understanding the Tantric traditions of the Kathmandu Valley. 


The Sarvāmnāya also re-enters India, where it becomes one of the primary frameworks through which the rest of Classical Śākta tantra gets reintegrated into high-level esoteric Śrīvidyā practice at elite levels of initiation. Additionally, the Sarvāmnāya framework preserves a number of other distinctive Śākta traditions, especially the cult of Siddhalakṣmī, whose tantras have otherwise vanished from the subcontinent. In this tradition we will also include ritual manuals and texts specifically associated with the sacred geography of the Kathmandu Valley.
