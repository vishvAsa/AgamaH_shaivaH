The youngest of the classical traditions of Śākta Tantra, the veneration of the goddess Lalitā or Tripurāsundarī and her iconic Śrīyantra is the most prevalent tantric tradition in the world today. Emphasizing the aesthetic beauty of the supreme goddess, as opposed to her more ferocious aspects aligned with the culture of the cremation ground, Śrīvidyā introduces a new way of thinking about the organization of tantras—in terms of sections on pūjā, mantra, and cakra—and a new and deeply influential approach to the encoding of mantra. 

This subdivides the baseline fifteen-syllable (pañcadaśī) main mantra into three subcategories of kūṭa mantras (Vāgbhava, Kāmarāja, and Śakti). As they assume increasingly subtler forms, these core mantras can then be converted into higher mantra expressions such as the Kāmakalā, which are then increasingly consciously mapped onto the production and sustaining of higher states of consciousness. 

The different branches of Śrīvidyā are then in turn subdivided according to whether the initial kūṭa mantra begins with the letter “KA,” “HA,” or “SA,” with the so-called Kādi transmission representing the dominant approach. 

After higher levels of initiation, following a Pūrṇadīkṣā / Pūrṇābhiṣeka—such as the Śāmbhavadīkṣās and the Saparyās—the use of the Pañcadaśī mantra is replaced by Mahāṣoḍaśī, and the form of the Śrīyantra and its number of enclosures (āvaraṇa) become increasingly elaborate.

What we call “Śrīvidyā” emerges out of the coming together of a range of originally distinctive traditions. These include a cult of the god Kāmadeva—associated with love magic—as presented in the Nityākaula and Vāmakeśvarīmata; the Pratyabhijñā philosophy of the Non-dual Śaivism of Kashmir, integrated into the Yoginīhṛdaya and then the commentarial tradition beginning with Śivānanda; and the veneration of a cycle of Nityā goddesses associated with the phases of the moon as taught in the Tantrarājatantra. 

Both the theology and the practice of most forms of Śrīvidyā are also heavily indebted to the Trika, especially the Ekavīrā cult of the goddess Parā, and to the Kubjikā tradition, whose Saṃvārtamaṇḍala contributes to the shape of the Śrīyantra itself. 

More fundamentally, Śrīvidyā provides the common ritual language, the mode for presenting mantras, and the styles of textual presentation that most early modern Śākta traditions take as their model. Through works such as the Saubhāgyaratnākara of Vidyānandanātha, it has also redefined how ritual is taught within the genre of the paddhati.

## daxiNatA
In the early modern period and into the contemporary period, Śrīvidyā has thrived as the primary face of purity-conscious esoteric ritual associated with the Śaṅkarācārya orders and their Smārta disciples. It is also out of this context that the stotras dedicated to the tantric goddess Tripurā, such as the Saundaryalaharī and the Lalitāsahasranāma, have become part of wider popular culture, a unique development among the classical Śākta tantras. 

As reflected in works like the Śrīvidyārṇava, Śrīvidyā has also come to operate as the central medium through which more archaic forms of classical Śākta tantra are preserved in the form of higher-level initiations transmitted within Śrīvidyā adept networks. 

Less scholastic forms of Śrīvidyā are also integral to the majority of non-elite and non-brāhmaṇa forms of Śākta worship rooted in textual resources, such as is found among the Nātha yogīs. 

Finally, in the form of the reformed Kaulism of Bhāskararāya and his disciples, Śrīvidyā has been presented as offering a new reconciliation of Kaula tantra with Advaita Vedānta.
