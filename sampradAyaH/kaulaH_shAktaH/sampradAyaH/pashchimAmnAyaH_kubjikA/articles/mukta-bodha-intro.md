The most extensive surviving corpus of classical Śākta tantra belongs to the “crooked” goddess Kubjikā. Many of these texts were composed in the western Deccan, in what is now southern Maharashtra and Goa. Deeply indebted to the scriptures of the Trika, the worship of Kubjikā incorporates the “Ancient” tradition’s mantra system into its own distinctive visualization of the octagon-shaped Saṃvartāmaṇḍala, supplementing it with its own nyāsas and the iconic “pañcaprāṇava” mantras.

One particularly distinctive feature of the Kubjikā tantras is their rich narrative traditions. The texts repeatedly recount how the goddess assumed her iconic “crooked” form, while also relating her changing relationship with Bhairava over many ages. 

For the first time in the tantric traditions, we are also presented with numerous stories about the descent of particular siddhas into the human world. Such tales would have a profound influence upon the emergence of the Nātha yogis, who also inherit from the later Kubjikā tantras the distillation of earlier, more diversified representations of the yogic subtle body into the six-plus-one cakra system with Kuṇḍalinī at its center—no longer treated as an extension of ritual liturgy—that is most familiar to us today.
