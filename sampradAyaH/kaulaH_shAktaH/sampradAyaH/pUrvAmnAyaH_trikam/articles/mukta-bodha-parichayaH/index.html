Trika

Perhaps the oldest surviving form of Tantric Śāktism, the original name of this transmission is the “Pūrva,” or the Ancient Tradition. Though it has largely been eclipsed by other forms of Classical Śākta Tantra, the Trika’s reputation today is largely founded upon the fact that it provides much of the scriptural foundation for the Non-dual Śaivism of Kashmir.

One of its most important scriptures, the Mālinīvijayottara, is the explicit commentarial focus of Abhinavagupta’s exegetical ritual manual, the Tantrāloka, which presents itself as a guide to both the theology and practice of the Pūrva as a system.

krama integration

Following his guru Śambhunātha, Abhinavagupta is strongly influenced by a series of late Trika scriptures, such as the Trikahṛdaya and the Vīrāvalī, that taught an explicitly non-dual version of the tradition in which external ritual practice is substantially replaced by mental visualizations.

In these texts, the Ancient Tradition’s original triad of the goddesses Parā, Parāparā, and Aparā, along with the goddesses Mālinī and Māṭṛkasadbhāva—each embodying the letters of the Sanskrit language presented in diverging sequences—were complemented by the integration of the worship of the Kālī of the Krama.

Except in citations from the Kashmiri exegetes, however, this highly esoteric form of the Trika has not been preserved. Nor do we have direct access to the once equally influential part of the tradition, also frequently cited in the Tantrāloka, dedicated to Triśirobhairava.

mantra focus

What survives today are several early Trika tantras that either primarily present the early ritual system, such as the Siddhayogeśvarīmata and the Tantrasadbhāva, or, like the Mālinīvijayottara and the Vijñānabhairava, are mostly concerned with yogic visualization practices. What these texts share is a distinctive theology that emphasizes the capacity of mantras and language both to release and to bind human beings, and the power of the human imagination as a means of achieving liberation from transmigration.

Parātrīśikā

Another important work, which received a commentary (now lost) by Somānanda and one by Abhinavagupta (vivaraṇa), is the Parātrīśikā, which focuses exclusively upon the Goddess Parā, particularly her sonic nature as the root mantra SAUḤ. This Trika scripture became foundational for a corpus of Trika works composed in South India beginning in the twelfth century, and is sometimes distinguished by the appellation Parākrama or Ekavīrā.

Texts in this tradition include the corpus of Madhurāja, who was from Madurai, Tamil Nadu, and his son Kṛṣṇadāsa’s Parātrīśikālaghuvṛttivimarśinī.

There is also the Parātrīśikātātparyadīpikā composed in Cidambara, the Anuttaraprakāśapañcāśikā of Ādyanātha, the Parāprāveśikā (alias Svarūpaprakāśikā) of Nāgānanda with a commentary (vimarśinī) by Cidānanda, an anonymous Anuttarasaṃvidarcanācarcā, the Parārcanakrama included in the Aghoraśivācāryapaddhati, the Parākramavāsanā of Śāmbhavānanda, the Parāpaddhati in the Nityotsava of Umānanda, and the Anuttarapaddhati of the Paraśurāmakalpasūtra.

The fact that some of these Trika materials are housed in Śrīvidyā works invites future research on the relationship between these traditions during this period.

Non-dual Śaivism of Kashmir

trika & krama roots

Emerging from the literature of two prominent Śākta traditions that flourished in Kashmir, the Trika and the Krama, a body of texts developed that distanced themselves from the particular ritual and mantra systems of their scriptural background while translating and articulating a non-dual view of selfhood, cosmology, and corresponding practices.

This “translation” of Śākta scriptural traditions, particularly in their Kaula phase, involved a more outward-facing and generalized Śaiva teaching, even while theologically and phenomenologically elaborating the Goddess-centered doctrines and principles of the Trika and the Krama.

shivasUtra and spanda

An early text to take this step toward a broader Śaiva non-dual vision was the Śivasūtra, revealed to Vasugupta. The Spandakārikā, likely composed by his disciple Kallaṭa Bhaṭṭa, is another early non-dual Śaiva text of this kind.

Commentaries

and both garnered a significant number of commentaries, which expanded this literature from its less elaborate sūtra and kārikā-based beginnings.

These texts were less focused on cosmology and metaphysics as such, and they dispense with references to mantras and rituals of initiation. Instead, they initiate a significant process of internalization of tantric ritual and yoga, streamlining them into more direct awareness practices (although the Śivasūtra preserves some elements of classical tantric yoga in its third chapter).

somAnandAdi

The next major phase of non-dual Śaivism in Kashmir comes with the development of a lineage of texts that are progressively more philosophical: the Śivadṛṣṭi of Somānanda, and the extraordinary corpus of his disciple Utpaladeva, followed by the commentaries of Abhinavagupta.

While Somānanda initiates this philosophical turn and outlines some of its major contours, Utpaladeva’s Īśvarapratyabhijñākārikā fully establishes this non-dual Śaiva philosophy as a śāstra, whose ideas become foundational to later tantric exegesis within and beyond Kashmir. This corpus of texts is also involved in a process of translating the views and cosmologies of Śākta scriptural traditions, but does so for an expanded audience by utilizing the discourse of Sanskrit dialectics, epistemology, and pan-Indian methods of debate.

shrIvidyA input

Beginning in the seventeenth century, a collection of Sanskrit texts emerges from a lineage of Śrīvidyā initiates who made significant contributions to the earlier tradition of non-dual Śaivism that flourished in Kashmir, while creatively drawing from many other early modern trends in Śākta, Vedānta, and Bhakti traditions. The most prolific and creative author of this lineage is Sāhib Kaula, whose writings have been studied, translated, and contextualized by Jürgen Hanneder.

These texts include stotras (e.g., Śārikāstotra, Śivajīvadaśaka, Sahajārcanaṣaṣṭikā), paddhatis (Śrīvidyānityapūjāpaddhati, Śyāmāpaddhati, Hṛlekhāpaddhati), and the Devīnāmavilāsa, a literarily complex reworking of the Bhavānīsahasranāmastotra, whose third chapter paraphrases the entire Pratyabhijñāhṛdaya of Kṣemarāja.

Many of these works include ritual and devotional worship of Northeastern Goddesses (Dakṣiṇakālī, Bhuvaneśvarī, Tārā), which the Kaula lineage may have been responsible for first introducing into the region of Kashmir.

Sāhib Kaula’s most unique work, unprecedented in Sanskrit literature, is the Kalpavṛkṣa, which is the subject of a monograph by Jürgen Hanneder. This work is difficult to categorize, since it visually maps multiple textual sources and integrates them into a single text that includes citations moving in multiple directions across a lattice-like structure (similar to the European carmina cancellatum) and incorporates “intexts” from thirty languages. The overarching purpose that Sāhib Kaula assigns to this work is to demonstrate how “Advaita Vedānta, Śaiva non-dualism, and supreme non-dualism are all the final conclusion of all philosophical systems.”

This de-emphasis on doctrinal difference, in order to allow for a harmonization of Advaita Vedānta with forms of non-duality that previously flourished in the Kashmir Valley, is part of a larger transregional trend.

Two examples are the complex rapprochement taking place between Śrīvidyā and Vedānta during this period and the integration of Śākta concepts and practices with general non-dual Vedānta teachings implicit in much of the Haṭhayoga literature associated with the Nāthas, which James Mallinson refers to as a “fortuitous union of non-dualities.”

In addition to Sāhib Kaula’s corpus, there is a body of stotras and ritual paddhatis composed by the next generation of this lineage—Jyotiṣprakāśa, Cidrūpa Kaula, Gaṇeśabhaṭṭāraka, and Sadānanda Kaula—as well as by Jyotiṣprakāśa’s son, Govinda Kaula. These works include a corpus of gurustotras and hymns to Tripurasundarī and Bhairava.

Finally, the writings of Bhāskarakaṇṭha, a contemporary author, can also be included in this tradition, particularly his sub-commentary (Bhāskarī) on Abhinavagupta’s Īśvarapratyabhijñāvimarśinī, the

Mokṣopāyaṭīkā, and the Cittānubodhaśāstra. Another important author of the eighteenth century is Śivopādhyāya, who composed the Vijñānabhairavavivṛti.
