Trika Śaivism didn't originate in Kashmir, nor was it first expounded by Abhinavagupta. The Trika is an ancient Śaiva turned Kaula tradition spanning multiple geographies. Abhinavagupta received the transmission from his Trika guru, Śrī Śāmbhunātha who was visiting Kashmir (likely taking shelter from the Islamic invasion) from Jālandhara Pīṭham—not to be confused with the modern day city. Śambhunātha's Trika guru, Sumati, was from "dakṣiṇabhumipīṭha" (likely referring to the Deccan), although Jayaratha claims there was another guru (Somadeva) in between them, but he was mistaken. In this sense, Abhinavagupta's own Trika lineage was not at all from Kashmir, but the South.

Abhinavagupta reformulated many aspects of the earlier Trika tradition and brilliantly synthesized it with his previous training (initiation) in the Kālīkrama to develop his unique and unprecedented understanding of the Trika Śaiva darśana.

Previous Trika śāstras, which Abhinavagupta cited extensively include the Siddhayogeśvarīmata, Trikasadbhāva, Trikahṛdaya, Triśirobhairava, and the Mālinīvijayottara, among others. What is outstanding is that despite the proliferation of these tantras, his stated goal in writing Tantāloka was to write a paddhati that preserved and explained the entire darśana because one did not exist.

Abhinavagupta's understanding of Trika is known as Anuttara Trika, and is the culmination of all the previous forms of the Trika.
