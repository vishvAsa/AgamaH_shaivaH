## Trans-regionalism
Though often thought to be equated with the region of Karnataka and the language of Kannada, the traditions we now call Vīraśaiva were, from their inception, multiregional and multilingual. As both a source of scriptural authority and a medium for ritual and philosophical expression, over the centuries Sanskrit has played an important role in shaping these traditions. 

## Roots
Both the Vīraśaivas and their earlier Vīramāheśvara predecessors inherit many key values, concepts, and practices not only from the Śaiva and Śākta Āgamas, especially the Trika, but also from otherwise lost pre-tantric Atimārga scriptural corpora, such as the Vātula.

Indeed, it is these pre-tantric resources that provide these traditions with such defining practices as the bearing and worship of a personal liṅga (iṣṭaliṅga), as well as their critique and rejection of caste norms. 

### trika-influence
Vīraśaivism is also considerably influenced by the Non-dual Śaivism of Kashmir. This is reflected in the vocabulary of texts from the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries composed in the western Deccan, such as the Anubhavasūtra and the Viśeṣārthaprakāśikā, but is also an integral feature of early modern Vīraśaiva scholasticism. 

The Śivādvaitamañjarī, for example, seeks to reconcile the advaitas of the Śivasūtras and Brahmasūtras, while Mariṭoṇḍarya’s commentary on the Siddhāntaśikhāmaṇi complements its reading of a root text that already draws deeply on Trika theology with citations from Utpaladeva and the Parātrīśikā.

## sthalas
Such reflections on the roots of Sanskrit Vīraśaiva literature should not blind us to the rich originality of the tradition itself, which through programs of practice such as the six stations (ṣaṭsthala) and 101 stages (ekottaraṣaṭsthala) innovates comprehensive programs for integrating a variety of modes of ritual worship with varying levels of individual understanding about the relationship between the devotee and Śiva.
