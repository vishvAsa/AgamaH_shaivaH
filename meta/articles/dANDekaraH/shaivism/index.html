RGB’s Vedic Rudra theory

Compared to Vaiṣṇavism, the origin of Śaivism is more obvious and its historical evolution less complex. According to RGB, the beginnings of Śaivism have to be traced back to the Vedic god Rudra who personified the dreadful and destructive phenomena of “the storms that uproot trees and even demolish houses, accompanied by the thunderbolt which strikes down men and beasts dead in a moment and the epidemics that rage and carry off numbers of men”.1 Rudra (from the root rud ’to howl’) went about howling with the stormy winds (Maruts) who were his sons (Rudriyas). RGB further adds that these dreadful phenomena were the result of the wrath of Rudra, which, however, could be appeased with prayer, praise, and offering. Rudra then became Śiva or the benignant one. In the ṚV, Rudra stood for the destructive agency of lightning. But when he was duly propitiated with prayers, he became paśupā ‘protector of the cattle’ (I. 114. 9). Rudra caused diseases, but he also possessed the healing remedies (I. 43. 4). This ambivalent character of Rudra was often emphasized in the ṚV. The conception of the Vedic Rudra gradually developed further until, as seen in the Śata-rudrīya (TS IV. 5. 1; VS 16), he became the god “of wild and awful scenes, such as cemeteries, mountains and forests. Of the beasts and savages that dwelt in these last and of the thieves and outcasts that resorted to them, he became the lord”.2 Subsequently he developed into the god who pervaded the universe, dwelt in fire and water, in all beings, and in herbs and trees, and thus became the supreme ruler of all. Indeed, as pointed out by RGB, [[P264]] even in the ṚV, Rudra was represented as having risen to supreme power (VII. 46. 2). It was in this form that the god became “the subject of Upaniṣad speculation, by meditating on whom and seeing whom everywhere in the universe a man attained blissful serenity”.3

This theory put forth by RGB is characterised by the two common assumptions of early Indology, namely, (1) that the origin of everything Indian has ultimately to be traced back to the Veda and (2) that the Vedic gods are basically personifications of natural phenomena. It, however, needs to be emphasized that the real popular religion, which was essentially distinct from the Vedic religion, remained outside the purview of the Veda. The Veda, it must be realised, does not teach us everything on ancient social and religious conditions of even Aryan India. It may be said to have emanated from a narrow circle of poet-priests, and reflects a somewhat singular view of things.4 Long before the advent of the Vedic Aryans, there had prevailed in various parts of India widely spread and deeply rooted popular religious cults. Though they might have apparently suffered a kind of setback on account of the onslaught of the freshly vigorous Vedic way of life and thought, some of them proved influential enough to compel the sponsors of Vedism to accommodate them, in one form or another, in the framework of their religious ideology. And when, in the course of the religious history of India, Vedism began gradually to decline, these popular religious cults again came into their own and confirmed themselves as the religion of the people. Such, in broad outline, is the genesis of Vaiṣṇavism, Śaivism, and other minor religious sects which together constitute the major part of the classical Hinduism. It has been rightly observed that these religious sects are of a fairly ancient date though their positive history is comparatively modern.

Vedic Rudra

How a pre-Vedic non-Aryan religion was adopted into the Vedic religious ideology is perhaps best illustrated by the [[P265]] personality and character of Vedic Rudra.5 From a cursory study of the various references to Rudra in Vedic literature, one generally gets the impression that two distinct Rudras are presented in that literature – the Ṛgvedic Rudra,6 who is a heavenly god with Maruts as his hosts, and the post-Ṛgvedic Rudra, who is earthly in character and is associated with evil spirits and dreadful beings. However, the basic identity of these two Rudras is quite unmistakable. A critical consideration of the name, the outward appearance, and certain special features of these two Rudras would leave no doubt about this. The distinction between the two Rudras is only apparent and is occasioned by varying emphasis.

Appearance, ambivalence, and ritual

It is averred that the Vedic gods, in contrast with the Greek gods for instance, lack concreteness and ’lifefulness’; their characterisation tends to become stereotyped and conventional, and, therefore, pale and colourless. Rudra does not play any very important role in Vedic mythology and ritual; and yet, more particulars are given in the Veda about his personality and character than about those of many other gods. As has been rightly pointed out, Rudra has ‘more body’ than the others. He is shown as possessing a distinctive and more or less well-defined individuality. He is clearly isolated from other Vedic gods in respect of appearance, dress, and other paraphernalia. For instance, he is said to be brown (babhru: II. 33. 5) or red in complexion (VS 16. 7). He is blue-necked (VS 16. 7) and blue-tufted (AV II. 27. 6); his belly is black and his back red (AV XV. 1. 7–8). He is kapardin (I. 114. 1, 5) and wears a multiform necklace (niṣka) as his special ornament (II. 33. 10). He is also described as being clothed in a hide (VS 3. 61; 16. 51). The Aitareya-Brāhmaṇa (V. 2. 9) speaks of Rudra as appearing in a dark dress.

[[P266]] Rudra is essentially ambivalent in character (which fact is duly noticed by RGB). He is described as a fierce god (II. 33. 9, 11; X. 126. 5), destructive like a terrible beast (II. 33. 11), unassailable and unsurpassed in might (I. 114. 4; II. 33. 3). Mention is frequently made of Rudra’s death-bringing missiles (II. 33. 10). As a matter of fact, fear of Rudra’s terrible shafts and deprecation of his wrath and ill will may be said to be the ruling sentiments of a large number of Ṛgvedic passages concerning Rudra (II. 33. 4–6, 15). Rudra’s very name is frightening (II. 33. 8). It is to be never directly mentioned. In the post-ṚV Vedic literature, Rudra’s malevolence and wrathfulness are more frequently referred to and deprecated (VS 3. 61; AV XI. 2). According to the Aitareya-Brāhmaṇa (III. 33), Rudra is the compound of the terrible forms of the gods, while the Śata-patha-Brāhmaṇa (IX. 1. 1, 6) says that even gods are afraid of him. But Rudra is not exclusively malevolent and maleficent. For, he is said to bestow blessings on his worshippers (I. 114. 1, 2; II. 33. 6) and generally look after the welfare of man and beast (I. 43. 6). He sees to it that the inhabitants of a village are well-fed and free from disease (I. 114. 1). But by far the most significant reference in this connection is the one to Rudra’s great healing powers. He is the physician of physicians (II. 33. 4), whose remarkable remedies are often praised by the Ṛgvedic poets (I. 114. 5; II. 33. 7, 12; V. 42. 11; VII. 46. 3). Rudra’s healing powers are referred to also in the post-ṚV Vedic literature (AV II. 27. 6; VS 3. 59; 16. 5, 49), but less frequently than his frightful and destructive nature. This ‘double personality’ of Rudra seems to have been regarded by the Ṛgvedic poets as the special distinguishing feature of that god. A brahmodya stanza (VIII. 29. 5) indirectly speaks of Rudra as a god, who is at once ferocious (ugra) and bright and holy (śuci), and who wields in his hand a sharp weapon (tigma āyudha) and at the same time possesses marvellous healing powers (jalāṣa-bheṣaja).

Similarly, on the one hand, Rudra is called the ruddy boar of the heaven (I. 114. 5), or the great Asura of the heaven (II. 1. 6), while, on the other, the Śata-rudrīya associates him with various [[P267]] kinds of people and places on the earth. He is glorified as the lord of thieves, pilferers and plunderers (VS 16. 20–22). Not only this; he is himself characterised as robber and prowler (even in ṚV I. 114. 4, Rudra is referred to as vaṅku kavi), cheater and deceiver (ibid).7 In the Śata-rudrīya, Rudra is presented with all the characteristics of purely popular origin – as a figure so real in popular consciousness. As so appositely observed by BARTH, “we can conceive nothing more life-like than the figure which stands out from this piece of rude realism, but nothing, at the same time, less Brahmanic”.8 In this unusual litany, we hardly come across any expression bearing on ritual or any allusion to a sacred custom. Rudra certainly does not appear here as a sovereign god, but there are clear indications that he must have originally been one.

Rudra does not enjoy any position of prominence and honour in the Śrauta ritual. He is either ‘driven back to his home’ as in the Agnihotra,9 or only the remnants of oblations are assigned to him.10 As against this, Rudra plays a particularly important role in certain gṛhya or popular rites such as the Śūla-gava,11 the Āśvayujī, and the Pṛṣtakā. Thus, while Rudra is generally excluded from the homas offered in hierarchical ritual, he is very positively represented as being entitled to the balis offered in folk-cult rites.

On account of all such features, Rudra stands out almost segregated from the generality of Vedic gods.12 Notwithstanding this the Veda cannot be said to exhibit any open opposition [[P268]] to Rudra-worship as such. There is no doubt that the Rudra-cult was entirely alien to the Vedic milieu, but, under the pressure of certain peculiar circumstances, it had to be accommodated, howsoever haltingly, into the Vedic religious complex.

Cultic affiliations

Attention may now be drawn to certain other characteristics of the Vedic Rudra. In the Ṛg-veda, Rudra is closely associated with Maruts. It would, however, seem that the hosts of Rudra were originally called Rudras, that, when Rudra was admitted into the Vedic pantheon mainly as god of death, he was deliberately brought into relationship with Maruts who had originally been independent divinities and were regarded as the hosts of the spirits of the dead, and that this relationship of Rudra with Maruts, which was frankly tendentious, proved to be but short-lived. Rudra’s affinity with Agni, on the other hand, is perhaps more constant and enduring. Indeed, in some passages, Rudra seems to have been actually identified with Agni (II. 1. 6; AV VII. 87. 1; TS V. 4. 3. 1). But this is obviously just incidental and not essential. The word rudra was primarily used as an epithet of Agni, but later it tended to be interpreted as another name of Agni. It is also natural that the terrific form (ghorā tanūḥ) of Agni should be referred to as rudra (TS II. 2. 2. 3). The legend that, on a certain occasion, Agni wept and was, therefore, called Rudra (TS II. 2. 10)13 is patently an afterthought. On the contrary, it is not improbable that, since, on account of his peculiar character and associations, Rudra’s name was taboo among the Vedic poet-priests, he was often represented as concealing under the names of Agni.14 And, if Rudra was really identical with Agni, how could one explain Rudra’s peculiar position in the hieratic Vedic ritual?15 As against this, the points of [[P269]] similarity between Rudra on the one hand and Śarva and Bhava (VS 16. 18, 28), Paśu-pati (frequently in VS and AV: RGB connects this name—not convincingly—with the Ṛgvedic epithet paśupā), Tryambaka (VS 3. 58), and post-Vedic Śiva are particularly vital and intrinsic.

But it is Rudra’s unmistakable connection with certain religious cults of ancient times which throws a very suggestive light on the essential personality and character of that god. The Keśin hymn (X. 136), for instance, relates to the orgiastic cult of the Munis who are said to have worn long hair, indulged in a sort of ecstasy-producing potion, and attained certain miraculous powers which were comparable to those attained through Yoga; and, in that hymn, Rudra is represented almost as the leader of that cult (X. 136. 7). The cult of the Brahma-cārins is glorified in AV XI. 5.16 These Brahma-cārins practised severe penance and thereby became endowed with peculiar virile powers which enabled them to undertake even cosmic activities. The description of the Brahma-cārin tends to remind one of Vedic Rudra. Similar is the case of the Vrātyas (AV XV) who had developed their own distinctive religious ideology, social order, and general way of life. Vedic Rudra betrays many points of contact with their supreme god whom they called Eka-vrātya.17

Among the features of the Vedic Rudra which differentiate him from the major Vedic gods must also be mentioned his association with what may be broadly called the demon-world, with the serpents, and, as indicated above, generally with non-social, non-divine, fear-inspiring elements. It is, however, not correct to conclude on the strength of such references, as RGB does, that “the sentiment of fear is at the bottom” of the worship of Rudra-Śiva.18

Storm-god vs. god of death

The most common theory regarding the character of Rudra is that he is the roaring storm-god who is both frightful and beneficent. RGB has endorsed this theory without any discussion, but specifying that Rudra represented the destructive agency of lightning. According to him, the simple folk working in the field saw in the black cloud tinged red by the soft glow of the lightning god Rudra with his blue neck and red complexion.19 In the present context, it is neither possible nor necessary to subject this theory to any critical examination. Suffice it to say with OLDENBERG that, in the Vedic descriptions of Rudra, lightnings do not flash, rains do not pour down, winds do not rush forth.20 Therefore, at least in the consciousness of the Vedic poets, Rudra was not a god of tempest.21 Moreover that theory miserably fails to explain, rationally and satisfactorily, the complex personality of Rudra with its distinctive and exclusive features as set forth above. As for Rudra’s identification with Agni, it has already been shown to be just incidental and not essential.22 According to OLDENBERG,23 who considers Rudra’s connection with mountains and forests to be far more vital and essential than that with storm and lightning, characterises that god as the Old Man of the Mountains, a demon of disease coming from forest and mountain, like Mars Silvanus. But, while emphasizing one significant aspect of Rudra’s character,24 OLDENBERG has completely ignored other equally or [[P271]] even more important aspects. ARBMAN25, like OLDENBERG, rejects the theory that Rudra is the god of thunderstorm or that he personifies any other phenomenon of nature. According to him, Rudra is an unheavenly product of lower mythology, an earthly, demonic, fearful god who has originated from the primitive concepts of death and its fears. He further asserts that Ṛgvedic Rudra represents an artificial side-growth of the continuous line of development from the original primitive non-Vedic Rudra to the later Vedic Rudra, and then to the epic Śiva.

There is no doubt that in the Vedic literature, particularly in the RV, greater emphasis is put on Rudra’s association with death and its horror than on any other aspects of his complex personality and character. Rudra’s sāyakas (II. 33. 10) are the death-bringing arrows shot by Rudra, the sender of disease and death. In I. 114. 10, Rudra’s missiles are unequivocally characterised as ‘cow-slaying’ and ‘man-slaying.’ In IV. 3. 6, that god is directly called nṛhan, the slayer of men. A reference has already been made to the significance of the relationship which was tendentiously established between Rudra and Maruts (who originally represented the spirits of the dead), after that god had been admitted to the Vedic pantheon.26 Rudra’s character as the god of death is further concretised, so to say, in later Veda. In the AV, for instance, he is very significantly described as assailing his [[vicitms|victims]] with diseases (XI. 2. 22, 26; VI. 90; 93). Rudra’s doubles, Bhava and [[Sarva|Śarva]], also are addressed in that Veda as gods of death (AV XI. 2. 2). Various details of the Vedic [[P272]] ritual in which Rudra figures confirm his character as god of death. In the Sākamedha, for instance, there is a funeral ceremony which includes an offering to Rudra. Incidentally it may be pointed out here that, according to the primitive belief, the god of death and his hosts also bestowed fertility on land, animals, and human beings. It also needs to be added that, since the entire concept relating to Rudra was foreign to the Vedic ideology, the character of that god was not clearly defined in the Veda, and the emphasis often shifted from one aspect of his character to another. That is why Rudra cannot be said to have been represented in the Veda as god of death either exclusively or consistently.

The following points will now have been clear from the foregoing discussion: (1) that Rudra was essentially different from the normal hieratic Vedic gods; (2) that he belonged to a milieu which was foreign to the Vedic poet-priests; (3) that the Vedic poet-priests must have accommodated him in their religious complex under the pressure of circumstances, and, therefore, reluctantly and almost under protest; (4) that, while doing so, they either suppressed or transformed many of that god’s essential traits; and (5) that, as a result of this, only one aspect of Rudra’s original complex character, namely, that of the god of death came to be generally emphasized in the Vedic literature. One does, however, get a few glimpses of Rudra’s original complex character from various stray references to that god in the Veda. Indeed, some indications are available in the Vedic literature itself of the rise of Rudra, in the form of Rudra-Śiva, to the position of supreme godhead. For instance, various references in AV XV suggest Rudra’s correspondence with Eka-vrātya (XV. 1) and with Bhava, Śarva, Paśu-pati, Ugra Deva, Mahā-deva, and Īśāna (XV. 5). And, already in the Maitrāyaṇī-Saṃhitā II. 9. 1, Rudra is identified with Puruṣa and Mahā-deva.

Proto-Indian Śiva-Paśupati

There is sufficient evidence to justify the assumption that Rudra is but the Vedic version of a well-established pre-Vedic non-Aryan popular god. The religious cult centering round this proto-Indian god had spread far and wide — different aspects of [[P273]] that god and different forms of his worship having been emphasized in different regions of the country. The essential common basis of that religion is, however, quite unmistakable. This popular proto-Indian religion differed from the Vedic religion in many significant respects. For instance, that religion was iconic, that is to say, the followers of that religion worshipped their god in the form of some concrete symbol which was installed either in the open or within some temple-like structures which were usually erected away from the main settlements. The Vedic religion, on the other hand, generally did not know of any temple or image-worship. Perhaps the earliest suggestive literary reference to idols is to be found in Pāṇini V. 2. 101 (arcā: also pratikṛti in V. 3. 96: also cf. V. 3. 99). Idol-worship, accordingly, seems to have come into vogue among the followers of Brahmanism not very long before Pāṇini’s time. It may be suggested that, with the beginning of the movement for revitalising and popularising Brahmanism which [[crystalised|crystallised]] in the Sūtra-Vedāṅga period, several elements of the popular proto-Indian religion came to be assimilated into the Brahmanic religion, idol-worship being perhaps one of such elements. The proto-Indian worship consisted mainly of pūjā, that is besmearing the icon of the god with some sacred substance, and Bali, that is, the offering, made directly to the god, of raw flesh and blood. This mode of worship thoroughly contrasted with the Vedic ritual or homa in which usually cooked oblations (or Soma) were offered to the gods, not directly but through the sacrificial fire, in the accompaniment of Mantras. Like idol-worship, the word pūjā and also the religious practice denoted by that word were adopted by Brahmanism during the Sūtra-Vedāṅga period — and that too presumably for the very reason for which idol-worship was adopted. Some of the other features of the proto-Indian religion which clearly differentiated it from the Vedic religion were Yoga, penance and austerities, phallic cult, the god’s lordship over animals and association with serpents, fertility rites, importance given to Mother Goddess and to female divinities, and the propitiation of the spirits of the dead and their leader. To anticipate, most of these features are present, in one form or another, in the religious ideology relating to Vedic Rudra.

The “red god” and pūjā

The proto-Indian god was celebrated as ‘red god’ in some circles, particularly among the proto-Dravidians. They called him by the name Śiva (which proto-Dravidian word means the ‘red one’), because they worshipped him, among other things, by pouring over (Tamil pūcu from which the word pūjā is derived) his icon the blood of the animal, more particularly the buffalo (or, in some cases, even the human being), which was offered to him, and, as a consequence of this, the god actually looked red. Vedic Rudra has borrowed not only the name but also some characteristics of the red god. The word, rudra, has to be derived from a hypothetical lost root, rud, which must have meant ’to be ruddy or red’.27 Thus, Rudra, like proto-Dravidian Śiva, also means the red god. As pointed out earlier, Rudra is actually associated with the red colour in a variety of ways. He is described as being aruṣa (I. 114. 5), babhru (II. 33. 5), tāmra, aruṇa, vilohita, nīla-lohita (TS. IV. 5. 1; VS 16. 6, 7). Like the proto-Indian Śiva, Rudra also is offered Bali (and not homa) as in the Śūla-gava rite. Rudra’s fondness for blood is frequently referred to. The TS (II. 1. 7. 1 ff.), for instance, narrates the legend of vaṣaṭ-kāra breaking open the head of gāyatrī. On that occasion, we are told, the gods took the various juices issuing out of that head. But Rudra chose only the blood. The ĀpŚS (X. 13. 11) seems to refer to blood as Rudra’s water. In the Aśva-medha, the blood of the horse is assigned to Rudra (TS I. 4. 36), and, according to the Śata-patha-Brāhmaṇa (V. 3. 1. 10), Rudra longs for the cows slaughtered in the royal palace in the Rāja-sūya. Red colour is also suggestive of death, magic, evil omen, swearing, cursing, misfortune, etc. And it can be shown that both proto-Dravidian Śiva and Vedic Rudra were intimately connected with these concepts implied by the red colour.

When the Vedic poet-priests felt constrained by the force of circumstances to admit the popular proto-Indian red god to [[P275]] their pantheon, they naturally sought either to suppress or to transform many of his characteristic features. For instance, they retained the name of the god, but in a different form. They called him Rudra instead of Śiva. As has been pointed out earlier, it was the frightful, demonic aspect of Rudra’s character that was specifically stressed in the Vedic literature. This fact made his name almost a taboo. The Vedic poet-priests tried to get over this contingency by referring to that god euphemistically.28 They gave him the epithet śiva, which in Sanskrit meant ‘gracious’, ‘benevolent’.29 Several other Vedic gods also were characterised as śiva, but it was only in the case of Rudra that the epithet became, in course of time, the proper name of the god. It is not unlikely that this happened under the influence of what may be called folk-philology. For, the word śiva was phonetically similar to the name of the proto-Dravidian god (who was the direct ancestor of Vedic Rudra) but which (in Sanskrit) denoted just the opposite of what Rudra generally represented in the Veda. The name Śiva as such seems to be unknown to the Saṃhitās (except perhaps in ṚV VII. 18. 7 where Śiva seems to be the name of a tribe), the Brāhmaṇas, and the old Buddhist literature. It is only in the Śvetāśvatara-Upaniṣad that the word is first used as a proper noun. Thereafter both Rudra and Śiva began to be used as the names of one and the same god. Incidentally it may be added that Pāṇini mentions Śiva as a proper noun (IV. 1. 112), but he does not mention god Śiva by the side of Indra, Varuṇa, Bhava, Śarva, and Rudra (IV. 1. 49).

Indus proto-Śiva

There are indications that the Indus valley religion was related to the proto-Dravidian Śiva-religion: both were iconic; both sponsored phallus-worship; the buffalo played a special role [[P276]] in both; both served as the fountain-head of the religion of epic Śiva. In the pre-Vedic Indus valley civilization, the proto-Indian god was represented as ithyphallic Yogīśvara Paśu-pati. The famous Mohenjodaro seal,30 for instance, shows “the God, who is three-faced, is seated on a low Indian throne in a typical attitude of yoga with legs bent double beneath him, heel to heel, and toes turned downwards. His arms are outstretched, his hands, with thumbs to front resting on his knees…The lower limbs are bare and the phallus seemingly exposed…Crowning his head is a pair of horns meeting in a tall head-dress. To either side of the God are four animals, an elephant and tiger on his proper right, a rhinoceros and buffalo on his left. Beneath the throne are two deer standing with heads regardant and horns turned to the centre….”31 It is needless to add that all the typical features of this god are evidenced in the conception of Vedic Rudra and epic Śiva.32 As has been already pointed out, Rudra is connected with Yoga through his association with the Muni-cult while epic Śiva is often glorified as yogīśvara. The hieratic Vedic attitude towards Yoga and related practices, like renunciation, austerities, and wandering mendicancy, is reflected in Indra’s antagonism to the Yatis (TS 6. 2. 7. 5; Ait. Br. VII. 28). The Indus valley religion, like the classical Śiva-religion, promoted the worship of the anthropomorphic ithyphallic god as well as of the separate phallic symbol. The description of some of the Vrātyas as śamaṇīcameḍhra and of the Brahma-cārin as bṛhac-chepa tends to establish Vedic Rudra (who was associated with these cults) as representing a significant stage in the almost unbroken continuity from the Indus valley Paśu-pati to epic Śiva.33 As in the case of [[P277]] Yoga, so too in the case of phallic worship, the attitude of Vedism was contentious. The śiśna-devas (phallus-worshippers) were regarded as the enemies of the Vedic Aryans.34

The Indus valley god’s lordship over animals is clearly suggested by the Mohenjodaro seal. Vedic Rudra (as also epic Śiva) is actually called Paśu-pati (VS 16. 17, 40), while most of the animals shown on the Mohenjodaro seal are brought into relation with epic Śiva in a special manner.35 The Indus valley Paśupati’s tall head-dress perhaps reappears in the form of Rudra’s uṣṇīṣa (VS 16. 22) and kaparda (VS 16. 43). And though, neither Vedic Rudra nor epic Śiva is represented with horns, there is hardly any doubt that the horns do reappear in the form of the crescent moon which Śiva bears on his forehead.36 Another Indus valley seal37 provides evidence for the assumption that the serpent cult was an integral part of the Indus valley Śiva-religion. Vedic Rudra also is associated with serpents in various [[P278]] ways. For instance, AV III. 27 and VI. 56. 2–3, which are said to pertain to Rudra, are regarded as serpent charms.38

Apart from the proto-Dravidian Śiva and the Indus valley Paśu-pati (whose identity will have now become quite manifest), two other forms of the same proto-Indian god are referred to in the Śata-patha-Brāhmaṇa (I. 7. 3. 8) – Śarva39 who was glorified by the Eastern people and Bhava who was glorified by the Vāhīkas.40 It is significant that both these regions lay outside the brahmanised country.

Thus Rudra is not a storm-god as RGB supposes, but represents just the Vedic version of the proto-Indian Śiva-Paśu-pati. It is to this latter god, and not to Vedic Rudra (as RGB says), that the origin of Śaivism is to be traced back. Indeed, most of the essential features of Śaivism are anticipated by the proto-Indian religion of Śiva-Paśu-pati and consequently by the religious complex pertaining to Vedic Rudra.41

Brāhmaṇas, Pāṇini, and Kauṭilya

I have suggested42 that the word śivāsaḥ occurring in ṚV VII. 18. 7 may denote the people who worshipped Śiva as their chief god.43 The passage mentions various tribes who tried to [[P279]] win over Indra to their side – of course, hypocritically – against the Vedic Aryan leader Sudās in the Dāśarājña. The Viṣāṇins, that is, the people wearing a head-dress adorned with horns (presumably, like their god depicted on the Mohenjodaro seal) are mentioned side by side with the Śivas. This may be understood as confirming the character of the Śivas as the worshippers of Śiva. It would appear that the Śivas, the Viṣāṇins, and the Śiśna-devas, who are represented in the ṚV as the enemies of the Vedic Aryans, professed the same proto-Indian Śiva-religion. Apart from this solitary and not improbable reference, the word śiva does not occur in the Saṃhitās or the Brāhmaṇas in the sense of either god Śiva or the worshipper of Śiva. As pointed out by RGB,44 in the Śata-patha-Brāhmaṇa VI. 1. 3. 7 and the Kauṣītaki-Brāhmaṇa VI. 1, 9, Rudra is referred to as the son of Uṣas. Prajā-pati is said to have given him eight names, seven of which are the same as those mentioned in AV XV. 5. 1–7 while the eighth is Aśani. More significant is the reference to Rudra roving from place to place, hankering after the sacrificer’s cattle and then being propitiated by an offering on the cross-road (Śata-patha-Brāhmaṇa II. 6. 2. 6). It may also be noted that the cult of the Vrātyas receives special attention in the Pañcaviṃśa-Brāhmaṇa, while the reference to Muni Aitaśa in the state of ecstasy (Ait. Br. VI. 5. 7) and to Keśin Dārbhya (Kauṣ. Br. VII. 4) remind one of the Muni cult. All this does not, however, imply any particular development in the Rudra-Śiva religion as reflected in the Brāhmaṇas. To suggest, as WEBER does,45 on the strength of the Kauṣītaki-Brāhmaṇa ascribing the epithets īśāna and mahān deva to Rudra, that the beginnings of the sectarian Rudra-worship are thereby indicated does not seem to be warranted.46 Nor is sufficient evidence available in the Brāhmaṇas to justify KEITH’S observation47 that [[P280]] in the period of the Brāhmaṇas the old polytheism was declining and Śaivism was gaining ground.

Pāṇini refers in IV. 1. 49 to the gods Bhava, Śarva, and Rudra, without, however, hinting, in any way, their essential identity; but, as pointed out earlier, he does not seem to know of Śiva as the name of a god.48 Pāṇini IV. 1. 49 explains the formation, among others, of such names as Bhavānī, Śarvāṇī, and Rudrāṇī, from Bhava, Śarva, and Rudra respectively, denoting the wives of those gods.49 The name Śivānī, derived similarly from Śiva, occurs in classical Sanskrit literature, but Pāṇini does not mention Śiva in that Sūtra. According to some scholars,50 the āyaḥśūlika implied by P. V. 2. 76 is a kind of Śaiva ascetic, but the wording of the Sūtra51 does not seem to support such an assumption.52 Megasthenes mentions two gods who had become known to him in India – Hercules of the plains and Dionysos of the mountains. Hercules is usually identified with Kṛṣṇa, and it is suggested that Dionysos may stand for Rudra-Śiva who is described as giriśa or giritra. But the essential character of Dionysos is that of a god of wine, and, as pointed out earlier, he is to be identified with Saṃkarṣaṇa-Baladeva rather than with Śiva.53 Nevertheless, soon after Pāṇini’s time, Śiva seems to have begun being recognised, by that name, as a prominent god of the people. For, Kauṭilya recommends the construction of a temple of Śiva in the centre of a city.54

Śvetāśvatara-Upaniṣad

The Śvetāśvatara-Upaniṣad (ŚU) represents a very significant landmark in the history of Śaivism. That Upaniṣad is in respect of Śaivism what the Bhagavad-gītā is in respect of Vāsudevism-Kṛṣṇaism. There is, however, no doubt that the Bhagavad-gītā is chronologically later than the ŚU. It can, indeed, be shown that many concepts occurring in the ŚU have been further developed in the Bhagavad-gītā.55 The ŚU (V. 2) mentions Kapila, the founder of the Sāṃkhya system,56 who may be taken to have belonged to the 6th century B.C. That would fix the terminus ante quem non for the origin of that Upaniṣad. Further, from the grammatical point of view, that Upaniṣad is clearly post-Pāṇinian.57 The ŚU may accordingly be assigned to about the end of the 5th century B.C. Conceptually, the ŚU may be placed between the Kaṭha-Up. and the Bhagavad-gītā.58

Monotheism and Sāṃkhya-Yoga

The ŚU is essentially monotheistic in character. Eko devaḥ59 – these words may, indeed, be said to be the key-words of this philosophical tract. This One God is beyond all contingency and variation, and yet he is the creator as well as the destroyer – the Urgrund – of the world. He also sustains everything: bharate viśvam īśaḥ (I. 8). It is verily through his greatness that this brahmacakra is set in motion (VI. 1). He is described as being immanent (I. 16; II. 16) as well as transcendent (III. 10, 14) – a kind of polarity which is pregnantly expressed and which deeply touches the heart.60 And, manifestly, this eka deva is Rudra:

[[P282]] eka eva rudro ‘vatasthe na dvitīyo ya imāṃl lokān īśata īśanībhiḥ (III. 2).61 He is now definitely identified with Śiva (IV. 14, 15, 18), and is also referred to as Hara (I. 10), Maheśvara (VI. 7), Bhagavat (V. 4), Giriśanta – Giritra (III. 5, 6), and Mahān Puruṣa (III. 8). His description as vṛkṣa iva stabdhaḥ (III. 9) seems to suggest his name Sthāṇu. In its glorification of Rudra-Śiva as the One God, the ŚU is evidently influenced by the concept of Eka-vrātya (AV XV) and the descriptions in the Śata-rudrīya.62 Verily, the ŚU represents a kind of philosophical continuation of the ancient Śata-rudrīya.

Rudra being thus represented in the ŚU as the Supreme Being, it is difficult to agree with RGB’s observation that this Upaniṣad “does not appear to be composed in a sectarian spirit”.63 Again, Rudra in the ŚU is not, as RGB seems to suggest,64 the result of the personalisation of the impersonal Brahman. There are clear indications in the Upaniṣad that the God is actually superior to Brahman – he is brahmayoni (V. 6) and brahmapara (III. 7). It is true that Sāṃkhya (VI. 13), Yoga (VI. 13), and Vedānta (VI. 22) – all these three are mentioned by name in the ŚU, but the teachings of that Upaniṣad are basically Sāṃkhya-Yoga-oriented,65 the Vedāntic elements in [[P283]] them, if any, being only superficial.66 The word māyā occurring in this Up. is, for instance, to be understood not in the strict Śāṃkara-Vedāntic sense of illusion, but rather in the Ṛgvedic sense of the māyā of Asura Rudra – the devātma-śakti and its creation. The Upaniṣad thus seeks to invest life with some meaning and value as it were. In the ultimate analysis, however, the general tendency of the Upaniṣad is, like that of the Bhagavad-gītā, to bring about a kind of philosophical synthesis.

Bhakti and grace

Mokṣa is naturally the goal according to the ŚU, and it is to be attained by means of the knowledge of the One God: jñātvā devaṃ mucyate sarva-pāśaiḥ (II. 15). The Upaniṣad does not seem to tire of repeating this dictum (IV. 14–15). But far more important in this connection is Bhakti for god Rudra-Śiva. Indeed, as has been rightly observed, the most characteristic feature of the religious sects of India, in general, is that they subordinate knowledge to a psychical fact of a totally different nature, namely, Bhakti, that is, faith, humble submission, absolute devotion, love for God, without which knowledge is either vain or impossible.67 It has been already pointed out that the cult of Bhakti, which was presumably pre-Vedic and non-Aryan in origin, [[P284]] must have come to be revived some time before Pāṇini’s time.68 It is but natural that the Bhakti-cult should have been specifically related, first of all, to the god who had been known to the people from very early times, namely, Rudra-Śiva. As required by the doctrine of Bhakti, the worship of Rudra-Śiva was open to all people irrespective of race, varṇa, or āśrama.69 The ŚU emphasizes that it is through meditation on,70 application to, and identification with Hara that the unreality, that the world is, finally comes to an end: tasyā ‘bhidhyānād yojanāt tattva-bhāvād bhūyaś cā ’nte viśva-māyā-nivṛttiḥ (I. 10). And even the fact that Śvetāśvatara is enabled to impart his esoteric teaching to a person who has the deepest Bhakti for the God and for the Guru71 is itself the result of his tapas but more particularly of the grace of God (VI. 21, 23).72

Later Śaiva Upaniṣads

A few other Upaniṣads are characterised as Śaiva,73 but most of them belong to a late date. A brief reference may, however, be made here to some significant points from a couple of these Upaniṣads. The Atharva-śira-Up.,74 which has some verses common with the ŚU, opens with the gods’ query regarding the nature of Rudra and Rudra’s definite assertion that he is the one eternal god and that none besides him exists. Among Rudra’s various names mentioned in this Upaniṣad, attention may be specially drawn to Bhagavat and Mahā-deva (44). The Upaniṣad also speaks of the Pāśupata-vrata (67), the bhasma-dhāraṇa-vidhi (which is a typically Śaiva practice) (67), and the paśu-pāśa-vimokṣa (68).

[[P285]] The Kaivalya-Up. (2. 5) glorifies the study of the Śata-rudriya as the source of true philosophical knowledge: yaḥ śata-rudriyam adhīte so ‘gnipūto bhavati… anena jñānam āpnoti saṃsārārṇava-nāśanam. Similarly, the Jābāla-Up.75 (3) declares that the murmuring of the Śata-rudriya leads to amṛtatva. The concluding portion of the Pañca-brahma-Up. characterises Śiva as sac-cid-ānanda and saṃsāra-mocaka. The Maitrī-Up., which, strictly speaking, is not a Śaiva Upaniṣad, is nonetheless important in that it seems to refer to different Śaiva sects and their practices (VII. 8). The Kena-Up., which presents Umā Haimavatī (consort of Rudra-Śiva) as explaining to Indra the mystery (yakṣa) that is Brahman (III. 12; IV. 1), may also be incidentally mentioned in the present context.76

Śiva in the Mahābhārata

It is particularly noteworthy that, though the Mahā-bhārata (MBh.) is generally conceived in the interest of Vaiṣṇavism (Vāsudevism-Kṛṣṇaism), Kṛṣṇa being represented almost as the primo motore of the Great Epic, the cultus which is often quite emphatically glorified in the epic is that of Śiva. One would, therefore, be justified in assuming that, long before the MBh. assumed its final form, the Śiva-cult had re-established itself as a dominant force in the religious history of India. The two major characters of the epic, namely, Kṛṣṇa and Arjuna, are represented to have been the special proteges of Śiva. They are shown to have survived unscathed the horrendous onslaught which Aśvatthāman had mounted against the Pāṇḍava army after the death of his father. When asked by Aśvatthāman how that could have happened, Vyāsa narrated to him how formerly Nārāyaṇa had practised [[panance|penance]] on the Maināka mountain for 60,000 years, how, as the result of it, he had seen Rudra-Śiva appear before him, how he had then praised Śiva as the highest god, and how, finally, Śiva had granted to him the boon of invincibility in battle. [[P286]] The privilege of this favour of Śiva was shared also by Nara who was born of Nārāyaṇa’s tapas. Nārāyaṇa and Nara, who, in the epoch of the MBh., incarnated themselves as Kṛṣṇa and Arjuna respectively, continued to enjoy that favour of Śiva (Droṇa. 172). A similar explanation was given by Vyāsa in another context. While Arjuna was routing the Kaurava forces, he saw that a resplendent person bearing śūla was actually doing so for him. When he reported this to Vyāsa, the latter told him that it was Śiva who had been constantly guarding him throughout the war. Vyāsa then praised Śiva with what is referred to as a caturvidha stotra (Droṇa. 173).77 Verily, as described in the Āraṇyaka-parvan (38–41), Arjuna had obtained his strongest weapon, namely, the Pāśupata-astra, from Śiva himself whom Arjuna had propitiated through tapas and who, in the guise of a Kirāta, had subsequently put to test Arjuna’s fortitude and valour.78

In the first episode mentioned above, a hint was incidentally given by Vyāsa that Aśvatthāman also was in a way cherished by Śiva (Droṇa. 172. 82). This very point seems to have been further developed in the Sauptika-parvan (6–7). When, after having been temporarily dissuaded from his proposed nocturnal raid, Aśvatthāman returned to his camp, he saw a gigantic apparition blocking his way. He attacked that phantom with all his might, but to no avail. He suspected that the phantom was born from his own evil inclinations; he, therefore, surrendered to Śiva, sang him a hymn of praise, and then actually prepared to offer himself as an oblation to the God. Śiva was thereby propitiated. He then told Aśvatthāman of the rigorous austerities which Kṛṣṇa had practised for the sake of his favour and declared that no one was more beloved to him than Kṛṣṇa (Sauptika. 7. 61: tasmād iṣṭatamaḥ kṛṣṇād anyo mama na vidyate). Further, in the same parvan, it is narrated that Yudhiṣṭhira asked Kṛṣṇa how Aśvatthāman had, single-handed, dared perpetrate the atrocity [[P287]] during the night and that Kṛṣṇa replied that it was all due to the credulous favour of Śiva. Kṛṣṇa further took that opportunity to expatiate on Śiva’s greatness. Brahmā, he continued to tell Yudhiṣṭhira, implored Śiva to create the world and procreate the human race. Śiva agreed and undertook severe penance for that purpose. Having waited for the God for a long time, Brahmā decided to bring about creation through another agency. Śiva returned from his penance and saw that the world had already been created. Greatly enraged, he cut off his generative organ and violently planted it upon the earth, for, he thought, it was no longer needed. Kṛṣṇa also told Yudhiṣṭhira of the sacrifice of the gods in which no portion was offered to Śiva and which, therefore, Śiva had ruthlessly exterminated (Sauptika. 17–18).

Kṛṣṇa-Upamanyu and stutis

But Śiva’s unquestioned supremacy is perhaps best illustrated by the Kṛṣṇa-Upamanyu episode described in the Anuśāsana-parvan (14–18). Jāmbuvatī desired a son from Kṛṣṇa. Having known that Rukmiṇī had given birth to valiant sons as the result of Kṛṣṇa’s worship of Śiva, she importuned Kṛṣṇa again to worship that God for her sake. Kṛṣṇa went to the Himālaya mountain for penance and casually came to the hermitage of Upamanyu. Upamanyu asked Kṛṣṇa to practise penance there only, for, Śiva himself was residing there. Upamanyu then told Kṛṣṇa how he had practised penance in order to propitiate Śiva, how he had refused to accept boons from any god other than Śiva, and how, in the end, Śiva had manifested himself before him and had fulfilled all his desires (14). Kṛṣṇa, too, is then said to have propitiated Śiva through penance and to have thereby obtained the desired boons (15–16). This narrative is followed by the Śiva-aṣṭottara-sahasra-nāma-stotra (17) and the eulogy of that Stotra by different sages (18). In another context, on having been asked by Yudhiṣṭhira what specific knowledge he had obtained from Durvāsas,79 Kṛṣṇa responded by glorifying the supreme greatness of Rudra-Śiva (Anuśāsana. 145) and by explaining the significance of the great god’s various names, such

[[P288]] as, Maheśvara, Rudra, Mahā-deva, Śiva, Sthāṇu, Paśu-pati, etc. (Anuśāsana. 146). It is noteworthy that, in both these cases, a special mention is made of the Śata-rudriya (Anuśāsana. 145. 4; 146. 23).

Various occasions have been manoeuvred by the redactor of the MBh. to introduce—appositely or otherwise—hymns of praise of Śiva. Some of the more important of these Śiva-stutis are: by Kṛṣṇa in Droṇa. 172, by Vyāsa in Droṇa. 173, by the gods and the Brahmarṣis on the eve of the destruction of Tripura (Karṇa. 24), by Aśvatthāman in Sauptika. 7, by Upamanyu in Anuśāsana. 14, by Kṛṣṇa in Anuśāsana. 15, and the Śiva-aṣṭottara-sahasra-nāma-stotra (Anuśāsana. 17).80 It may also be mentioned that many tīrthas spread over different regions of the country are described in the epic as having been sanctified by association with Śiva, in one form or another—a fact that would indicate the wide sphere of the influence of the Śiva-religion.

Epic features of Śaivism

Attention may now be drawn to some characteristic features of the Śiva-religion which become evident from the many allusions to Śiva occurring in the epic. In the epic Śiva-religion, greater importance is attached to tapas than to Bhakti or any other form of worship. And this is in full conformity with the proto-Indian character of that religion. Whoever seeks to propitiate Śiva practises penance—for instance, Nārāyaṇa, Upamanyu, and Kṛṣṇa did so. Even Śiva, who is naturally enough represented as the supreme god, himself takes to tapas whenever he wants to accomplish something. That phallus worship is an integral part of the Śiva-religion is also frequently emphasized in the epic. Indeed, Upamanyu argues that Śiva is the greatest of the gods because it is only his liṅga—and of no one else—that is worshipped by gods (Anuśāsana. 14. 100–102: na śuśruma yad anyasya liṅgam abhyarcyate suraiḥ). The suggestion that liṅga here means merely a concrete symbol or an indicatory mark [[P289]] would not carry conviction. For, the symbols of even other gods are seen to be actually worshipped. But it is only in the case of Śiva that his generative organ is worshipped. That liṅga, in the context of Śiva, invariably signifies the generative organ is confirmed by the legend narrated by Kṛṣṇa to Yudhiṣṭhira regarding Śiva having cut off his liṅga when he discovered that it was no longer needed since the process of creation had already been accomplished (Sauptika. 17. 24). Again, the MBh. is definitely familiar with the circumstance that Śiva is worshipped in two different forms—in the human form and in the form of liṅga (Droṇa. 173. 94: pūjayed vigrahaṃ yas tu liṅgaṃ vā ’pi samarcayet; also Anuśāsana. 146. 15–17).

One is often reminded of Śiva’s ancient character as the god of fertility in respect of man, animal, and land, when one reads in the epic, not infrequently, of persons propitiating Rudra-Śiva for the sake of progeny (cf. Anuśāsana. 14–16). The almost unbroken continuity of the Śiva-religion from the proto-Indian period to the epic-paurāṇic period is further evidenced, among other things, by Śiva appearing as a Kirāta (Āraṇyaka. 38–41) or as a Puruṣa bearing śūla (Droṇa. 173) and by his being denied a share in the sacrifice (Sauptika. 18; Śānti. 274).81 Similarly, some of the ideological features of Śaivism which were first introduced in the ŚU are seen to have been delineated also in the epic—such as, for instance, its Sāṃkhya-Yoga orientation.82 Supreme godhead of Śiva, which is frequently emphasized in the epic, may also be mentioned in this very context. As for this last, however, it needs to be pointed out that, though Nārāyaṇa-Kṛṣṇa is usually represented as a protege of Śiva, the redactor of the epic, presumably with a view to stressing the Vaiṣṇava genesis of the epic, has introduced some episodes wherein Rudra-Śiva is [[P290]] said to be just a product of the wrath of Nārāyaṇa (Brahmā being the product of his grace)83 or Nārāyaṇa is shown to have seized Rudra by the throat84 or Rudra is made to surrender to Nārāyaṇa.85 But, even in such cases, the redactor either betrays his bias in favour of Śiva (Śānti. 328. 21; tasmād ātmānam evā ’gre rudraṃ sampūjayāmy aham) or seeks to establish a kind of equipoise between the two gods (Śānti. 330. 64: nā ’vayor antaram kiṃcin mā te bhūd buddhir anyathā).86

Liṅga worship

RGB’s treatment of phallus-worship is rather inadequate. And this is quite understandable, for, he did not have before him the considerable archaeological material which has become available in recent times. The tradition of phallus-worship in India is, indeed, very ancient. It has been suggested that the Neolithic people in South India were primarily worshippers of liṅga. Evidence is produced in this connection of the Kapagallu hill in Bellary district, where one sees a liṅga together with a bull in a crouching position.87 Be that as it may, there is certainly far more definite and far more profuse evidence regarding phallus-worship which has been unearthed in the Indus [[P291]] valley. The conical terracotta objects with rounded tops and the ringstones discovered in the Indus valley are, without doubt, the symbols of liṅga and yoni respectively. We have already seen that the chief god of the Indus valley people was the ithyphallic Yogīśvara Paśu-pati (who has been depicted on the Mohenjodaro seal and who is identical with the proto-Dravidian god Śiva).88 The ithyphallicism of the Mohenjodaro figure and the large number of liṅga-yoni-like objects discovered in Indus valley may be presumed to have been closely related to each other—verily, they have to be regarded as denoting the two aspects of the phallic worship which was an integral part of the proto-Indian Śiva-religion. It is not unlikely that the worship of the phallus, which, as evidenced by its almost universal dissemination, must have been a natural development in the history of human culture, had grown in India independently in the hoary past,89 and that it came to be associated with the religion of the ithyphallic god only at some later date. Whatever that may have been, one thing seems to be quite certain, namely, that, in the proto-Indian religion of the Indus valley period, the two had already become organically connected with each other. It is believed that an oblong terracotta sealing discovered in the Indus valley actually shows the figure of Śiva with his three symbols, namely, the bull, the trident, and the phallus.90 We may, therefore, assume that in the proto-Indian Śiva-religion the god was worshipped both in the anthropomorphic ithyphallic form and in the form of a separate liṅga. This practice of the worship of Śiva has continued even in historical times,91 though [[P292]] the liṅga-pūjā seems to have become more common in course of time.

As phallus-worship was supposed to have been unknown to the Veda, it was suggested that it had originated among the Dravidian races92 or among the Western nations and even among the Greeks.93 For one thing, it is not correct to say that phallus-worship was unknown to the Vedic poet-priests; indeed, they knew it and abhorred it as is indicated by their attitude towards the Śiśna-devas. And, secondly, in view of its universal diffusion in very ancient times, the question regarding its origin is rendered almost irrelevant.94

Non-phallic theories critiqued

There has been a tendency among Indian scholars—a tendency which is perhaps quite natural—to reject the assumption that the liṅga is a phallic emblem. It is suggested, for instance, that the idea of the liṅga originated out of an attempt to express the inexpressible, to make Śiva perceptible to the mental eye.95 The liṅga is Maheśvara himself, and not merely the emblem of his membrum virile (liṅgaṃ sākṣān maheśvaraḥ).96 It was also claimed that the liṅga signified a protest against idolatry. According to another interpretation, the Śiva-liṅga is the closest possible approximation to the cosmic Puruṣa—the hemispherical top of the liṅga consists of thousands of heads (sahasra-śīrṣan), each of the size of a point. The liṅga represents the double idea of the “formless” and the “all-formed” Śiva.97

[[P293]] But the more common idea which is presumed to underlie the liṅga is that of the axis mundi (which is also implied by the Atharvavedic Skambha and the Buddhist stūpa). Recourse is also taken in this connection to the Purāṇic account of the quarrel for precedence between Brahmā and Viṣṇu and the appearance before them of Śiva in the form of a blazing column the ends of which the two gods were asked to find but which, to their chagrin, they could not. This blazing column, it is pointed out, came to be later represented through the liṅga.98

Sculptural and literary evidence

The close relationship between the ithyphallic god and the liṅga-yoni-like objects, which is clearly implied in the Indus valley religion, should, indeed, put an end to all such speculations. There is also the strong corroborative evidence provided by the Guḍimallam liṅga of the second century B.C.99 whose similarity to the human male’s generative organ is quite unmistakable. And the still more pertinent point is that, at Guḍimallam, an anthropomorphic figure of Śiva, in standing position and with two arms, and the liṅga appear to be inseparably connected with each other.100 This would put beyond all doubt three facts, namely, (1) that the Śiva-cult and the liṅga-cult organically constituted a single religious complex, (2) that the liṅga signified the phallus, and (3) that, as in the proto-Indian period so too in historical times, Śiva was worshipped in two forms—in the human form and/or in the liṅga-form. The Bhita liṅga (first century B.C.), which, like Śiva, has five faces,101 and the many liṅgas with figures of Śiva belonging to the Kuṣāṇa period,

[[P294]] which have been discovered at Mathurā,102 would further confirm these facts if any such confirmation was at all needed. It is also noteworthy that Lakulīśa, the founder of the Pāśupata sect, who was often identified with Śiva himself, was sculpturally represented as seated in a yogic posture, adjoining a liṅga and at, the same time, with a prominent generative organ. Similarly, the Pāśupatas are known to have worshipped the liṅga as well as the mūrti of Śiva (Pāśupata-sūtra I. 6, 9).

As for the literary evidence in this connection, reference may be made to the description of Rudra-Śiva in the ŚU as the god, who, being one, rules over every single yoni (V. 2: yo yoniṃ yonim adhitiṣṭhaty ekaḥ), or as a person who is of the measure of a thumb103 (III. 13: aṅguṣṭha-mātraḥ puruṣaḥ; also V. 8); to Upamanyu’s unequivocal assertion (mentioned above) that Śiva was the greatest of all gods because it was only his liṅga which was worshipped;104 and to the Purāṇic account that the sages of the Pine Forest, accusing Śiva of seducing their wives in disguise, cursed him, that, as the result of the curse, the generative organ of Śiva dropped down, and that Śiva, revealing himself to them, taught them, to worship his fallen liṅga.105 It may, however, be added that, in the Purāṇas as also elsewhere, we do come across passages wherein attempts are made to foist some mystic or philosophical meaning on the concept of liṅga. All such attempts have proved to be manifestly artificial and unconvincing. We [[P295]] may also incidentally note that, notwithstanding the wide diffusion of phallus-worship in India since very ancient times, the word liṅga in the sense of phallus is, strangely enough, not seen to have been used in early literature. The word occurs in that sense for the first time in the concluding portion of the Mānava-Śrauta-sūtra which presumably belongs to the third century B.C.106 Mānava-Śrauta-sūtra 11. 7. 1. 13 reads: sugupta-deśe go-carma-mātre [[‘sthansvanulipte|sthaṇḍile svanulipte]] vā liṅga-padmākṛtiṃ prakalpya prokṣya “mā no mahāntam” iti sthāpayed “namaḥ śambhave” ce ’ty arcayet. The fact that this whole section is called rudrajapasya vidhāna-kalpa107 and the specific context in which the sūtra occurs confirm that the word liṅga here means ‘phallus’.108 From the Mahā-bhārata onwards, the word liṅga in the sense of ‘phallus’ seems to have obtained wide currency.

Numismatic evidence

In view of what has been said above, RGB’s statement that the liṅga-worship had not come into use at the time of Patañjali109 cannot be said to be valid. No doubt Patañjali does not refer to liṅga-worship, but that is just because he has no occasion to do so. Similarly RGB’s contention that the liṅga-cult must have been unknown even in the time of Wema Kadphises (65–75 A.D.), because, while his coins show the figure of Śiva as well as his emblems, the trident and the bull, they do not have the liṅga represented on them,110 cannot bear scrutiny. For, there is the evidence of an uninscribed cast coin of circa second century B.C., which has on its reverse a tree in railing on the left and liṅga on a square pedestal on the right.111 A liṅga can be recognised on a pedestal between two different trees in railing on some Ujjain [[P296]] coins of the third-second century B.C. It is also found on one or two copper coins from Taxila of about the same period.112 Probably, there is a liṅga on a square pedestal in the centre of a punch-marked coin.113

Patañjali’s Mahābhāṣya

The few references to the Śiva-cult which we come across in the Mahā-bhāṣya of Patañjali clearly indicate that that cult had become firmly established long before his time. Commenting on P. V. 3.99,114 Patañjali seems to suggest that, even in the fourth century B.C., the Śiva-worship had become so common that the Mauryas got the images of gods like Śiva, Skanda, and Viśākha115 prepared and sold to the people. In another context, Patañjali apparently hints that Śiva did not formally belong to the hierarchical Vedic pantheon. In his commentary on Vārttika 2 of P. VI. 3. 26,116 he specifically mentions the dvandva compounds formed with the names of the gods Brahmā and Prajā-pati, Śiva and Vaiśravaṇa,117 and Skanda and Viśākha as not being covered by the category of what he calls loka-vedayoḥ devatā-dvandva. But perhaps the most significant reference in the Mahā-bhāṣya, from the point of view of the history of the Śiva-religion, is that to the Śiva-bhāgavatas.118 The Śiva-bhāgavatas must have constituted a specific sect of the followers of the Śiva-religion — the distinctive characteristics of that sect being Bhakti for Śiva119 and the bearing of an iron lance (trident) in the hand. According to [[P297]] Patañjali’s commentary on P. V. 2. 76, a Śiva-bhāgavata cannot be called āyaḥśūlika though he carries an ayaḥ-śūla in his hand. The word āyaḥśūlika is not to be understood in the literal sense, but only in the metaphorical sense. It denotes a person who takes recourse to harsh measures in order to accomplish some work which can be accomplished by milder methods. The context seems to suggest that the āyaḥśūlika was a kind of extortionist false ascetic, like the daṇḍājinika, but his connection with the Śiva-religion, if any, is not clear.

Coins, inscriptions, and Hiuen-Tsang

RGB’s exploitation of the epigraphic material for the reconstruction of the history of Śaivism is, as usual, perfect. The numismatic evidence for the prevalence of the Śiva-religion, particularly in the first couple of centuries before and after Christ, is also not inconsiderable. It is noteworthy that most of this evidence is derived from north-western India. For instance, a bronze seal, discovered by MARSHALL at Taxila and belonging to the first century B.C., shows the figure of Śiva and the legend śivarakṣitasa in Brāhmī and Kharoṣṭhī characters.120 The coins of the Ārjunāyanas (cir. 50 B.C.) and the Yaudheyas (cir. 150 A.D.) show the bull which is obviously a Śaiva emblem. The Audumbaras (end of the first century B.C.) issued their coins in the name of the god Bhagavat Mahā-deva. A reference has already been made to the coins of Wema Kadphises who called himself Māheśvara. One of the prominent Śaiva sects was called Māheśvara; but it is doubtful whether the term was used on Wema’s coins in the strict sectarian sense as suggested by RGB.121 A coin of the Kuṣāṇa king Huvishka (106–138 A.D.) is said to have a motif similar to that of the Mohenjodaro seal of Paśu-pati.122 The end of the Kuṣāṇa power synchronised with the rise of the Bhāraśivas in northern India (cir. 150 A.D.). As [[P298]] their name clearly indicates, the Bhāraśivas were staunch followers of the Śiva-religion. So were the Vākāṭakas who were matrimonially related to the Bhāraśivas, Gautamīputra, the son of the Vākāṭaka sovereign Pravarasena I, having been married to a daughter of the Bhāraśiva king Bhavanāga.123 The period of about two hundred years from 150 A.D., which was dominated by these two dynasties, saw a kind of upsurge of the Śiva-religion. The Guptas, who came to real power in the first decades of the fourth century A.D., exhibited a remarkable catholic spirit in the matter of religion. Epigraphic and numismatic evidence indicates that most of the Gupta sovereigns were devout Vaiṣṇavas or Bhāgavatas,124 but there is also evidence to show that they and their ministers respected and patronised Śaivism without any inhibition.125 The prevalence of Śaivism in northern India in the post-Gupta period may very well be inferred from the statements of Hieun-Tsang (second decade of the seventh century) referred to by RGB.126 The Chinese pilgrim, who mentions the Pāśupatas as many as twelve times, says that there were temples of Maheśvara at which the Pāśupatas worshipped; in one or two of them they even resided. And, at Varanasi, Hieun-Tsang found about ten thousand sectaries who honoured Maheśvara, besmeared their bodies with ashes, went naked, and tied their hair in knots. It may be added that, when Hieun-Tsang travelled in India, Buddhism was rapidly disappearing from the south, while Śaivism, and especially Jainism, were becoming the popular faiths in that region.

South Indian Śaiva Bhakti

It has already been seen that south India was the provenance and the stronghold of a prominent aspect of the proto-Indian Śiva-religion. The various other original popular religious cults of south India also showed many traits similar to those of [[P299]] the early Śiva-religion. Indeed, they all seem to have formed a more or less integrated religious complex under the supreme godhead of Śiva. It was, therefore, no wonder that Śaivism, in its new sophisticated form, found in south India a most suitable soil for a rapid and exuberant growth. What the religious situation in south India had been when Śaivism made its re-entry in its ancient home is not clear. Buddhism is known to have become firmly established in the Deccan by the third century B.C. It continued to flourish during the Sātavāhana period (cir. 200 B.C.–250 A.D.), as is evidenced by the various monuments, big and small, which are scattered in the valley of the Kṛṣṇā and its environs. There are also indications that Buddhism, Jainism, and Brahmanic Hinduism co-existed in the south during the early centuries of the Christian era. Soon, however, Buddhism gradually disappeared from the religious scene in that region, and, as elsewhere so too here, it was Śaivism which profitted most by the expulsion of Buddhism. Śaivism had thus to contend mainly with Jainism.

Śaivism in south India developed in two directions—āgamic and of Bhakti, primarily in the latter direction. And this brings us to the three stalwarts of this Bhakti movement, the famous Tēvāram Trio. According to the generally accepted tradition, the first two of the Tēvāram Trio, namely, Tirunāvukkarasar (Appar) and Tiruñānasambandar were contemporaneous (Appar being the older of the two) and lived during the reign of Pallava king Mahendravarman (610–630 A.D.), while the third, namely, Sundarar, lived during the reign of Pallava king Narasiṃhavarman II Rājasiṃha (690–728 A.D.). Though born in an orthodox Śaiva Vellāla family, Appar was attracted to Jainism in his early life and joined the monastery at Pāṭaliputra (near Cuddalore in South Arcot) as a monk. Later, when he was stricken by some abdominal disease which the Jaina Munis failed to cure, Appar is said to have become, through the intervention of his sister, the recipient of the grace of Śiva which brought him immediate relief. A staunch missionary in the service of Śiva thereby came into being in the person of Appar. Appar soon succeeded in [[P300]] converting Mahendravarman (who had been a confirmed Jaina) to Śaivism. He thus initiated, as it were, a glorious epoch for the Śiva-religion in south India—an epoch which lasted for nearly the next four centuries through the regimes of the Pallavas, the Pāṇḍyas, and the Coḷas.127 Most of the religious activity was centred round the many Śiva temples, big and small, and “the ninth century saw the establishment of the first monasteries which were to find the Śaivite regions of the South particularly favourable to their development.”128

Spread of Śaivism outside India

Java and Bali

A brief reference may be made in this very context to the expansion of Śaivism outside India. It is well known that the first centuries A.D. saw brisk maritime and commercial activity on the part of the Indians, mostly from southern and south-eastern India, and in some cases also from Gujarat, in South-East Asia and particularly in the Indonesian Archipelago (Dvīpāntara), which resulted in the foundation of Hindu or Hinduistic kingdoms there. Inscriptions from that region, dating from the 5th century A.D., many of which employ a script similar to the one used in Coromandel under the Pallavas, show that Sanskrit was used for official and religious purposes. They also reflect the deep penetration in that region of Hindu religion and Dharma-śāstra. However, the few sources that have become available for the first two or three centuries, that is from the 5th to the 7th century, relate mainly to the powerful Sumatran kingdom Śrīvijaya which was an important centre of Buddhism. It is only the inscriptions belonging to the eighth, and more particularly to the ninth, century that bring to light the Śiva-dominated religious scene in Java.129 Scholars have [[P301]] suggested that, though in that period the princes might have been Buddhist, the people at large were predominantly Śaiva. But the fact seems to be that, in this middle-Javanese period, Śaivism not only co-existed with Buddhism of the Mahā-yāna type, but the two actually coalesced with each other.130 Beginning from 930 A.D., there were produced in East Java several Sanskrit texts with Old-Javanese paraphrases which afford glimpses into the Javanese Śaivism. Among such works may be mentioned the Bhuvana-kośa, the Bṛhaspati-tattva, and the Gaṇa-pati-tattva. These works seek to bring about the unification of various Śaiva sects by emphasizing their common basic teachings and practices. The Vrati-śāsana, a tract of unknown origin, throws ample light on the Śaiva asceticism as practised in the Indonesian Archipelago.131 From the theological-philosophical point of view, the vast body of speculative literature principally in Old-Javanese represents a stage earlier than that of the Śaiva-siddhānta of south India.132

In Bali too, Śaivism and Buddhism seem to have been mutually complementary, but the figure of Śiva was certainly ever dominant. He was also called Rudra, Īśvara, Giripati, Pañca-mukha, and Mahā-deva; and the liṅga-pūjā was, of course, a common religious practice. The so-called Balinese Veda, written in a mixture of Old Javanese, Balinese, and Sanskrit loan-words, actually relates, in a large measure, to the Tantric ritual of Śaivism. The supreme god of the Balinese has been Śiva-sūrya or Śivāditya with whom are connected the complicated rites of Sūrya-sevana. Attention may be drawn, in this context, to the [[P302]] fact that Śiva and Sūrya are identified with—indeed, are merged into—each other even in some early Purāṇas.

In connection with the Śaivism in the Indonesian Archipelago, a special mention needs to be made of the concept of Bhaṭṭāra Guru. The concept is evidently the result of the tendency to glorify and deify the Guru or the preceptor. In the Śaiva tradition, the Guru is believed to be infused with the spirit of Śiva—indeed, he is Śiva himself.133 Agastya also plays a significant role in the Indonesian Śaivism. He is mentioned in the oldest East-Javanese tradition (760 A.D.), and the work entitled Agastyaparva (11th century) refers to Bhakti for Śiva.

Mainland Southeast Asia and Borneo

In Cambodia, the medieval princes seem to have been devout worshippers of Śiva. For instance, the Han Chey inscription of Bhavavarman, who conquered Fu-nan (6th century A.D.), opens with a maṅgala dedicated to Śiva. Similarly, Jayavarman II of the early 9th century A.D., is said to have introduced a new type of Tantric ritual for the worship of Deva-rāja (represented by a Śiva-liṅga) who came to be recognised as the tutelary deity of Kambuja from that time onwards.134

In Campā, king Bhadravarman is described in the inscription of the early fifth century A.D. as having dedicated a temple to Śiva at Mi-son under the name Bhadreśvarasvāmin. There is sufficient evidence to show that Śiva was worshipped in Campā both in the anthropomorphic and the liṅga forms. Indeed, most of the kings of that country are known to have occupied themselves with the installation and consecration of Śiva-liṅgas. Reference may also be made here to the Wat Phu hill or the Liṅgaparvata in Laos where a temple of Śiva had been constructed. In Malaysia, on the right bank of the river Bujang, there existed a Pallava-type Śaiva shrine belonging to the sixth or the seventh century A.D. Inscriptions on yūpas of the late fourth century [[P303]] A.D., discovered at Kutei and Muara-Kaman in Borneo (Barhiṇa-dvīpa of the Vāyu-Purāṇa?) speak of the prevalence of Śaivism under Mūlavarman, the son of Aśvavarman.

Central Asia

Śaivism seems to have penetrated into Central Asia much earlier. The Greek author Stobaeus quotes a passage from Bardasanes about the visit of an Indian to Syria at the time of Antoninus of Emesa (218–222 A.D.). This passage contains a striking reference to Ardha-nārīśvara. A mention may also be made of the so-called Tri-mūrti image with Śiva as the central figure (cir. 3rd century A.D.) from Akhun Dheri near Charsadda. But more ample evidence is available from the 7th and the 8th centuries. There is, for instance, the collection of Śaiva antiquities (7th–8th centuries A.D.) derived from Tagao and Gardez in Afghanistan. The figure represented on a wall-painting at Piandjikent in Tadjikistan, belonging to about the same period, is believed to be that of Śiva. Similarly, certain finds in Dandan-Uliq and neighbouring sites of Taklamakan desert in Eastern Turkestan have brought to light traces of the Śiva-cult and the Śakti-cult.135

Śaiva sects and schools

RGB’s discussion of the names and doctrines of the different Śaiva sects,136 often supported by epigraphic and numismatic evidence, is quite enlightening. However, a few more points on that subject may be added here. The Vāyu-saṃhitā of the Śiva-Purāṇa mentions137 four sects of the Śaivas or the Māheśvaras, namely, the Siddhāntins, the Pāśupatas, the Mahā-vrata-dharas, and the Kāpālikas. Guṇaratna, in his commentary on the Ṣaḍ-darśana-samuccaya of Haribhadra, also refers to the four sects, but he substitutes the Kāla-mukhas for the Kāpālikas of the Vāyu-saṃhitā. He also mentions various subsects such as Bharaṭa, Bhakta, Laiṅgika, and Tāpasa. Guṇaratna further makes the interesting observation that the Naiyāyikas are Śaivas while the Vaiśeṣikas are Pāśupatas. The name Mahā-vrata-dhara [[P304]] given by these two sources, though epigraphically attested as Mahā-vratin, does not seem to have been common, for, other names are suggested in its place, such as Kāruṇika-Siddhāntin by Vācaspati in the Bhāmatī, Kāṭhaka-Siddhāntin by Bhāskara in his commentary on the Brahma-sūtras, Kālāmukha by Yāmunācārya in his Āgama-prāmāṇya, and Kāla-vadana (or Kāla-damana?) in the Vāmana-Purāṇa.

The Vāyu-saṃhitā speaks of two classes of the Śaiva Āgamas: (1) śrauta or Vedic which are of innumerable kinds and (2) svatantra or non-Vedic (Siddhānta) which are of 10 or 18 kinds and are believed to have been composed by Śiva himself. The Śaiva Āgamas mention twenty-eight Yogācāryas of Śaivism, from Śveta to Lakulīśa, while Guṇaratna enumerates eighteen Ācāryas from Lakulīśa to Vidyā-guru.138

Pāśupatas, Kāpālikas, and Kālāmukhas

Lakulīśa, who, according to RGB, was a direct pupil of Śrīkaṇṭha (mentioned in the Mahā-bhārata) and a contemporary of Patañjali,139 is believed to have composed the Pāśupata-sūtras (consisting of five adhyāyas), and the seventeenth Ācārya, Rāśīkara, to have written a commentary on that work known as the Pañcādhyāyī-bhāṣya or the Pañcārtha-bhāṣya. The Pāśupata worship consisted of some abnormal practices: hasita-gīta-nṛtta-ḍuṃḍuṃkāra-namaskāra-japa-upahāreṇa upatiṣṭhet.140 The Lakulīśa-cult spread widely and quickly—Lakulīśa himself having come to be regarded as representing the emanated anthropomorphised concept of the potent cosmic role of Śiva141 and several Lakulīśa temples having been constructed.

The Kāpālikas are also known as Soma-siddhāntins (presumably because they specially worshipped Śiva together with Umā) [[P305]] or Śiva-śāsanas and are, according to the Kūrma-Purāṇa, veda-bāhya, though Rāmānuja mentions yajñopavīta as one of their six distinguishing marks. They seem to have been first mentioned in the Maitrī-Upaniṣad as persons who are not worthy of association because of their revolting practices.142 Yet, this sect had an extensive vogue, as is indicated by archaeological and literary evidence. The Śāṃkara-dig-vijaya mentions Ujjain as the stronghold of the Kāpālikas.143 The Kālāmukha sect, which is closely connected with the Kāpālika sect, is believed to have been founded by Kauruṣya and is generally regarded as Vāmācāra Pāśupata. It seems to have migrated from Kashmir to Karṇāṭaka where it wielded considerable influence from the 9th to the 13th century A.D.

Siddhānta-mārga and Kashmir Śaivism

The Śiva-religion sponsored by the Purāṇas is usually known as the Siddhānta-mārga. Its principal tenet is Bhakti for Śiva, and its ritual practices also are fairly sober. The followers of this school are the ordinary Śaivas. But the name Siddhānta is more commonly given to the Āgamic Śaiva doctrine of south India and sometimes also to what may be called the Vedāntic Śaiva doctrine of Kashmir.

There is some uncertainty about the schools of Śaivism in Kashmir and their literature. RGB says that “the Kashmir Śaivism has two branches, the Spanda-śāstra and the Pratyabhijñā-śāstra.”144 The authorship of the first is attributed to Vasugupta and his pupil Kallaṭa, while the founder of the Pratyabhijñā school is said to be Somānanda, the author of the Śiva-dṛṣṭi.145 However, in the introduction to his edition of the Śiva-dṛṣṭi,146 Madhusudan [[P306]] Kaul Sastri characterises Somānanda as the follower of the Tryambaka school of the Kashmir Śaivism. It is pointed out that Śiva himself, in the form of Śrīkaṇṭha, imparted this doctrine to Durvāsas who, in his turn, passed it on to Tryambakāditya. It was Tryambakāditya, who, in course of time, settled in Kashmir and formalised these teachings, which later came to be known after him. Somānanda, the author of the Śiva-dṛṣṭi, was the twentieth in the line of teachers from Tryambakāditya. It is further suggested that Kashmir Śaivism comprises three schools — Tryambaka (which is very much older than the other two), Spanda (which is derived from the Spanda-sūtra and the Spanda-kārikā), and Pratyabhijñā which is taught in the Śiva-sūtras — and is, therefore, called Trika.147 Incidentally, RGB does not mention the Spanda-sūtra at all; there is also the tradition that the names Spanda-sūtra and Spanda-kārikā denote one and the same work; but Kṣemarāja (11th century A.D.), in his Śiva-sūtra-vimarśinī, specifically mentions both the Sūtra and the Kārikā. With Utpala (author of the Īśvara-pratyabhijñā-kārikā, the Īśvara-siddhi, etc.), Lakṣaṇagupta, and Abhinavagupta (author of the Pratyabhijñā-vimarśinī, the Tantrāloka epitomising the 64 non-dualistic Tantras, the Paramārtha-sāra, etc.), Kṣemarāja, who was a pupil and cousin of Abhinavagupta and who wrote, besides the Śiva-sūtra-vimarśinī, works like the Pratyabhijñā-hṛdaya, the Spanda-nirṇaya, and the Uddyotas, belonged to a distinguished line of teachers of the Kashmir Śaivism.

Tamil Śaiva Siddhānta

It has already been pointed out that Śaivism of south India developed in two main directions — of Bhakti and of Siddhānta. The Śaiva Siddhānta of the South arose independently and may be said to have supplemented the devotional effusions of the Śaiva saints with doctrinal reinforcement. It is, for instance, said that in the Tiruvāśagam of Māṇikka Vāśagar (10th–11th century A.D.), which together with the Tevāram of the Trio [[P307]] mentioned above, is regarded as constituting the Tamil Veda, the doctrines of the Śaiva Siddhānta have fused into the passionate experience in the heart of a Śiva-bhakta. Actually, however, in that poem the element of Bhakti is far more dominating than that of Siddhānta. “Scarcely ever”, writes POPE about the Tiruvāśagam,148 “has the longing of the human soul for purity and peace and divine fellowship found worthier expression.” The Śaiva Siddhānta was originally expounded in the eighteen Āgamas to which were added, in course of time, the ten Yāmalas and a number of Upāgamas. This doctrine was later systematized by Maykandar Karulturai (13th century A.D.) in his Śiva-jñāna-bodham and by his pupil Arulananti Śivācārya in his Śiva-jñāna-siddhiyār. Maykandar, who is glorified as the father of Tamil Śaivism, is said to have belonged to the Golaki-Lakṣādhyāyi school of Śaivism. This school seems to have been an offshoot of the Mattamayūra sect, which was originally patronised by the Kalacūri-Cedi princes of Central India and which entered into the Tamil country in the 10th century A.D.

Vīraśaivas and Liṅgāyatas

Besides the Sanskrit Śaiva Siddhānta and the Tamil Śaivism there was yet another school of Āgamic Śaivism in the South, namely, that of the Vīra-śaivas or the Liṅgāyatas. Actually, the word liṅgāyata does not occur in any extant traditional Sanskrit work on the Vīra-śaiva religion except the Vīra-śaivācāra-pradīpikā where it occurs as liṅgāyita. The attempt to explain grammatically the word liṅgāyata as a Sanskrit word is not convincing.149 It is more likely that the word is a Kannaḍa tadbhava form of Sanskrit liṅgavantaḥ.150 The term Vīra-śaiva is more extensive and covers both the Ārādhyas (who continue to adhere to some Brahmanic saṃskāras and practices) and the Liṅgāyatas (who represent a kind of revolt against the Brahmanic orthodoxy).151 To the basic [[P308]] Vīra-śaiva doctrines of śakti-viśiṣṭādvaita (which is generally derived from the Kashmir śivādvaita),152 ṣaṭ-sthala (six steps to mukti),153 and aṣṭāvaraṇa (eightfold protective shield against the evils of māyā), the Liṅgāyatas may be said to have added their specific practices of pañcācāra (fivefold code of life) and tri-ṣaṣṭi-śīla (sixty-three rules of daily conduct).154 A mention may be made at this stage of the Śivānubhava-maṇṭapa – the religious assembly which is said to have been organized by Basava155 and to have been presided over by Allama Prabhu. It is believed that Vīraśaivism, particularly its ṣaṭ-sthala doctrine, received its final form through the discussions of the Śaraṇas in this gathering. It was again the Śivānubhava-maṇṭapa which proved to be the fountain-head of the remarkable Vacana literature.

Distinctive features of Śaivism

Attention may now be drawn to some distinctive features of Śaivism. It may be recalled that Vāsudevism-Kṛṣṇaism originated from and centred round a human hero who came to be deified in course of time. One of the purposes of the Avatāra-theory seems to have been to provide a kind of rationalisation – and also substantiation – for such godship of a human being. Śiva, on the other hand, has been conceived as a god from the very beginning. And moreover he is conceived essentially as an earthly god – as a god who, being ever present, is intrinsically interested and involved in the affairs of man. Naturally, therefore, Śaivism does not countenance any Avatāras of Śiva. The tradition that, in each Kalpa, Śiva appears along with his four disciples is obviously [[P309]] sectarian and conventional. Actually, Śiva is believed to operate either directly or through the medium of the Guru. The peculiar concept of the Guru, which, incidentally, is not of Vedic origin,156 constitutes one of the important characteristics of the Śaiva religious ideology. In a sense, the Guru-tradition is to Śaivism what the Avatāra-theory is to Vaiṣṇavism.157

Naṭarāja and the erotic ascetic

Paradox, it is rightly observed, is the very heart of the Śaiva mythology. For instance, Śiva is often represented both as creator and destroyer. He is even said to be that power in the universe which causelessly destroys and causelessly creates.158 But there is nothing inherently paradoxical in this. The creator is also the destroyer, neither in anger nor by caprice but by the very nature of his activity.159 This ambivalence of Śiva’s nature is best reflected in his dance which typifies the eternal rhythm and polarity which are at the root of the infinite creative process.160 Through the various movements in the course of his dance, Śiva brings into being the corresponding cosmic forces—his dance is, indeed, “a piece of animated symbolism.”161 Śiva, the Naṭa-rāja, through the sheer rapture of exuberant power, dances the creation into existence,162 but, through his frenzied tāṇḍava, he also destroys what he has created, in order that he may re-create it and thereby perpetuate the rhythm of the creative process.

But the apparent paradox in Śiva’s personality and character is far more emphatically expressed in his portrayal as an ithyphallic Yogin. Śiva’s sexuality, it needs to be noted, has mystical rather than libidinous implications. It is the manifestation of an [[P310]] upsurge of a kind of cosmic energy and life-force. Through his asceticism, Śiva is enabled to keep that creative energy or life-force under control. Śiva personifies the ideal which rejects both the licentious indulgence and the stifling continence and which promotes the controlled release of power. Tapas and Kāma may be diametrically opposed, but they are by no means mutually exclusive.163 Indeed, both are regarded as being of the nature of fire or heat. These two polar opposites, namely, asceticism and sexuality, are believed to lead to the same condition, namely freedom from contingency. It is wrong to bifurcate the personality of Śiva and argue that eroticism belongs to the Śiva of the Tantras and asceticism to the Brahmanic Śiva. This apparent coincidentia oppositorum – this concept of the erotic ascetic – is to be understood as the expression of the essential polarity of one single integrated whole. Śiva comprehends within himself, superbly balanced, the supreme aloofness and contemplativeness of Tapas as well as the hectic fervour and involvement of Kāma. Indeed, all opposites are beautifully reconciled in his personality and character.164

The inadequate understanding and appreciation of the essentially integrated character of Śiva and the consequent emphasis on one or another aspect of his superficially multiple nature have given rise to an intriguing variety of Śaiva sects—the Kāpālikas with their bloody and revolting aghora practices, the Vāmācāra-Tāntrikas with their orgiastic rites; the ecstatic Pāśupatas; the mystic Śaiva-Siddhāntins of Kashmir; the aggressive Vīra-śaivas; and the saintly Śaiva Bhaktas of south India about whose fine devotional poems it is said that in them is to be found the frankest and the profoundest delineation of the individual religious life with its struggles and dejections, its hopes and fears, its [[P311]] confidence and its triumph.165 Such evocation of heterogeneous responses by a single godhead constitutes yet another noteworthy feature of Śaivism.

Śakti and Ardhanārīśvara

It is perhaps on account of its erotic mysticism that we see in Śaivism a comparatively heavy preponderance of the female divinity. As indicated above, tendencies are evident even in the proto-Indian Śiva-religion towards the worship of the male and the female aspects of divinity in a joint divine sovereignty. In classical Śaivism, Śakti, the metaphysically derived cosmic potence of Śiva, was granted an independent status and a separate personality. Indeed, in the cosmogony of the Tantras, the role of the creator actually belongs to her—Śiva being represented as a mere immobile spectator.166 But, here too, in conformity with the usual tendency of Śaivism, the two poles, namely, the male and the female aspects of divinity, are re-united, through a balanced integration, to form the magnificent godhead of Ardha-nārīśvara.167 Kālidāsa has very poetically brought out the paradox of Śiva, who, being ever united with his consort, still is the foremost among the all-abjuring ascetics.168 It is further paradoxical, in a sense, that the lord of the Yogins and the ascetics should have been regarded as the originator and the [[P312]] presiding deity of drama and dance.169 But, obviously, this idea has its origin in the cosmic dance of Naṭa-rāja Śiva. Mention may be incidentally made in this very context of the tradition that the first fourteen sūtras of Pāṇini’s grammar, which form the basis of a part of his terminology, were revealed to him by Śiva himself.170

Social outlook and Vaiṣṇavism

Having originated as a religion of the people (rather than of hieratic circles) Śaivism has been more or less non-conformist in character in the sense that it generally did not make much of the traditional Varṇāśrama-dharma. It also promoted equality of sex. Some of the Śaiva sects, like the Liṅgāyatas, even went to the extent of denouncing veda-prāmāṇya, Brahmanic orthodoxy, image-worship, and the doctrine of transmigration. They removed the bar to widow-remarriage and strongly protested against child-marriage.

There is no doubt that Śaivism is more ancient than Vaiṣṇavism, but the latter is said to be more expansive, more attractive, and more favourable to community of worship and religious sentiment.171 Both Śaivism and Vaiṣṇavism sponsor a personal monotheism (as against the earlier pantheism), but ritual practices are perhaps more in evidence in Śaivism than in Vaiṣṇavism. While Śaivism regards the universe as the source of pain and misery – as the pāśa of the paśu – and, therefore, as worthy of being repudiated, Vaiṣṇavism looks upon it as an evidence of the lord’s omnipotence and, therefore, as worthy of being cherished. But it may not be valid to assert that, on account of this attitude of Vaiṣṇavism towards the universe, its contributions to arts and literature have been richer and more profuse than those [[P313]] of Śaivism.172 Śaivism is known to have dominated the religious scene till the 11th century A.D.; but thereafter Vaiṣṇavism, which had been infused with new life in respect of both philosophy and devotionalism by the Ācāryas beginning with Rāmānuja, started to look up. And, though, today, Śaivism may reckon more sanctuaries, it is Vaiṣṇavism which is generally in ascendancy. Whatever that may be, perhaps the greatest glory of the Śiva-religion is that it can justifiably boast of the longest continuous and living tradition among the religions of the civilised world.
