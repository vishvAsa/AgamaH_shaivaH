## RGB's Vedic Rudra theory

Compared to Vaiṣṇavism, the origin of Śaivism is more obvious and its historical evolution less complex. According to RGB, the beginnings of Śaivism have to be traced back to the Vedic god Rudra who personified the dreadful and destructive phenomena of "the storms that uproot trees and even demolish houses, accompanied by the thunderbolt which strikes down men and beasts dead in a moment and the epidemics that rage and carry off numbers of men".[^263_145] Rudra (from the root *rud* 'to howl') went about howling with the stormy winds (Maruts) who were his sons (Rudriyas). RGB further adds that these dreadful phenomena were the result of the wrath of Rudra, which, however, could be appeased with prayer, praise, and offering. Rudra then became Śiva or the benignant one. In the *ṚV*, Rudra stood for the destructive agency of lightning. But when he was duly propitiated with prayers, he became *paśupā* 'protector of the cattle' (I. 114. 9). Rudra caused diseases, but he also possessed the healing remedies (I. 43. 4). This ambivalent character of Rudra was often emphasized in the *ṚV*. The conception of the Vedic Rudra gradually developed further until, as seen in the *Śata-rudrīya* (*TS* IV. 5. 1; *VS* 16), he became the god "of wild and awful scenes, such as cemeteries, mountains and forests. Of the beasts and savages that dwelt in these last and of the thieves and outcasts that resorted to them, he became the lord".[^263_146] Subsequently he developed into the god who pervaded the universe, dwelt in fire and water, in all beings, and in herbs and trees, and thus became the supreme ruler of all. Indeed, as pointed out by RGB, [[P264]] even in the *ṚV*, Rudra was represented as having risen to supreme power (VII. 46. 2). It was in this form that the god became "the subject of Upaniṣad speculation, by meditating on whom and seeing whom everywhere in the universe a man attained blissful serenity".[^264_147]

[^263_145]: RGB's *Collected Works*, Vol. IV, p. 145.

[^263_146]: *Ibid.*, p. 222.

[^264_147]: *Ibid.*, p. 222.

This theory put forth by RGB is characterised by the two common assumptions of early Indology, namely, (1) that the origin of everything Indian has ultimately to be traced back to the Veda and (2) that the Vedic gods are basically personifications of natural phenomena. It, however, needs to be emphasized that the real popular religion, which was essentially distinct from the Vedic religion, remained outside the purview of the Veda. The Veda, it must be realised, does not teach us everything on ancient social and religious conditions of even Aryan India. It may be said to have emanated from a narrow circle of poet-priests, and reflects a somewhat singular view of things.[^264_148] Long before the advent of the Vedic Aryans, there had prevailed in various parts of India widely spread and deeply rooted popular religious cults. Though they might have apparently suffered a kind of setback on account of the onslaught of the freshly vigorous Vedic way of life and thought, some of them proved influential enough to compel the sponsors of Vedism to accommodate them, in one form or another, in the framework of their religious ideology. And when, in the course of the religious history of India, Vedism began gradually to decline, these popular religious cults again came into their own and confirmed themselves as the religion of the people. Such, in broad outline, is the genesis of Vaiṣṇavism, Śaivism, and other minor religious sects which together constitute the major part of the classical Hinduism. It has been rightly observed that these religious sects are of a fairly ancient date though their positive history is comparatively modern.

[^264_148]: Cf. BARTH, *The Religions of India*, 1882, Preface, xiv.

## Vedic Rudra

How a pre-Vedic non-Aryan religion was adopted into the Vedic religious ideology is perhaps best illustrated by the [[P265]] personality and character of Vedic Rudra.[^265_149] From a cursory study of the various references to Rudra in Vedic literature, one generally gets the impression that two distinct Rudras are presented in that literature – the Ṛgvedic Rudra,[^265_150] who is a heavenly god with Maruts as his hosts, and the post-Ṛgvedic Rudra, who is earthly in character and is associated with evil spirits and dreadful beings. However, the basic identity of these two Rudras is quite unmistakable. A critical consideration of the name, the outward appearance, and certain special features of these two Rudras would leave no doubt about this. The distinction between the two Rudras is only apparent and is occasioned by varying emphasis.

[^265_149]: For a comprehensive study of Rudra, see: DANDEKAR, "Rudra in the Veda", *Vedic Mythological Tracts*, pp. 199–277.

[^265_150]: So far as the number of hymns in which he is celebrated in the *ṚV* is concerned, Rudra will have to be regarded as occupying a very subordinate position in that Veda.

### Appearance, ambivalence, and ritual

It is averred that the Vedic gods, in contrast with the Greek gods for instance, lack concreteness and 'lifefulness'; their characterisation tends to become stereotyped and conventional, and, therefore, pale and colourless. Rudra does not play any very important role in Vedic mythology and ritual; and yet, more particulars are given in the Veda about his personality and character than about those of many other gods. As has been rightly pointed out, Rudra has 'more body' than the others. He is shown as possessing a distinctive and more or less well-defined individuality. He is clearly isolated from other Vedic gods in respect of appearance, dress, and other paraphernalia. For instance, he is said to be brown (*babhru*: II. 33. 5) or red in complexion (*VS* 16. 7). He is blue-necked (*VS* 16. 7) and blue-tufted (*AV* II. 27. 6); his belly is black and his back red (*AV* XV. 1. 7–8). He is *kapardin* (I. 114. 1, 5) and wears a multiform necklace (*niṣka*) as his special ornament (II. 33. 10). He is also described as being clothed in a hide (*VS* 3. 61; 16. 51). The *Aitareya-Brāhmaṇa* (V. 2. 9) speaks of Rudra as appearing in a dark dress.

[[P266]] Rudra is essentially ambivalent in character (which fact is duly noticed by RGB). He is described as a fierce god (II. 33. 9, 11; X. 126. 5), destructive like a terrible beast (II. 33. 11), unassailable and unsurpassed in might (I. 114. 4; II. 33. 3). Mention is frequently made of Rudra's death-bringing missiles (II. 33. 10). As a matter of fact, fear of Rudra's terrible shafts and deprecation of his wrath and ill will may be said to be the ruling sentiments of a large number of Ṛgvedic passages concerning Rudra (II. 33. 4–6, 15). Rudra's very name is frightening (II. 33. 8). It is to be never directly mentioned. In the post-*ṚV* Vedic literature, Rudra's malevolence and wrathfulness are more frequently referred to and deprecated (*VS* 3. 61; *AV* XI. 2). According to the *Aitareya-Brāhmaṇa* (III. 33), Rudra is the compound of the terrible forms of the gods, while the *Śata-patha-Brāhmaṇa* (IX. 1. 1, 6) says that even gods are afraid of him. But Rudra is not exclusively malevolent and maleficent. For, he is said to bestow blessings on his worshippers (I. 114. 1, 2; II. 33. 6) and generally look after the welfare of man and beast (I. 43. 6). He sees to it that the inhabitants of a village are well-fed and free from disease (I. 114. 1). But by far the most significant reference in this connection is the one to Rudra's great healing powers. He is the physician of physicians (II. 33. 4), whose remarkable remedies are often praised by the Ṛgvedic poets (I. 114. 5; II. 33. 7, 12; V. 42. 11; VII. 46. 3). Rudra's healing powers are referred to also in the post-*ṚV* Vedic literature (*AV* II. 27. 6; *VS* 3. 59; 16. 5, 49), but less frequently than his frightful and destructive nature. This 'double personality' of Rudra seems to have been regarded by the Ṛgvedic poets as the special distinguishing feature of that god. A *brahmodya* stanza (VIII. 29. 5) indirectly speaks of Rudra as a god, who is at once ferocious (*ugra*) and bright and holy (*śuci*), and who wields in his hand a sharp weapon (*tigma āyudha*) and at the same time possesses marvellous healing powers (*jalāṣa-bheṣaja*).

Similarly, on the one hand, Rudra is called the ruddy boar of the heaven (I. 114. 5), or the great Asura of the heaven (II. 1. 6), while, on the other, the *Śata-rudrīya* associates him with various [[P267]] kinds of people and places on the earth. He is glorified as the lord of thieves, pilferers and plunderers (*VS* 16. 20–22). Not only this; he is himself characterised as robber and prowler (even in *ṚV* I. 114. 4, Rudra is referred to as *vaṅku kavi*), cheater and deceiver (ibid).[^267_151] In the *Śata-rudrīya*, Rudra is presented with all the characteristics of purely popular origin – as a figure so real in popular consciousness. As so appositely observed by BARTH, "we can conceive nothing more life-like than the figure which stands out from this piece of rude realism, but nothing, at the same time, less Brahmanic".[^267_152] In this unusual litany, we hardly come across any expression bearing on ritual or any allusion to a sacred custom. Rudra certainly does not appear here as a sovereign god, but there are clear indications that he must have originally been one.

[^267_151]: Such descriptions can by no means be ignored as being merely fantastic.

[^267_152]: BARTH, *Op. cit.*, p. 162. C. V. NARAYANA AYYAR (*Śaivism in South India*) tries to reconstruct from such allusions in the *Śata-rudrīya* as *upavītin* (*VS* 16. 17: sacrificer-ruler-punisher) the social background of Rudra being conceived as 'fierce'.

Rudra does not enjoy any position of prominence and honour in the *Śrauta* ritual. He is either 'driven back to his home' as in the Agnihotra,[^267_153] or only the remnants of oblations are assigned to him.[^267_154] As against this, Rudra plays a particularly important role in certain *gṛhya* or popular rites such as the Śūla-gava,[^267_155] the Āśvayujī, and the Pṛṣtakā. Thus, while Rudra is generally excluded from the *homas* offered in hierarchical ritual, he is very positively represented as being entitled to the *balis* offered in folk-cult rites.

On account of all such features, Rudra stands out almost segregated from the generality of Vedic gods.[^267_156] Notwithstanding this the Veda cannot be said to exhibit any open opposition [[P268]] to Rudra-worship as such. There is no doubt that the Rudra-cult was entirely alien to the Vedic *milieu*, but, under the pressure of certain peculiar circumstances, it had to be accommodated, howsoever haltingly, into the Vedic religious complex.

### Cultic affiliations

Attention may now be drawn to certain other characteristics of the Vedic Rudra. In the *Ṛg-veda*, Rudra is closely associated with Maruts. It would, however, seem that the hosts of Rudra were originally called Rudras, that, when Rudra was admitted into the Vedic pantheon mainly as god of death, he was deliberately brought into relationship with Maruts who had originally been independent divinities and were regarded as the hosts of the spirits of the dead, and that this relationship of Rudra with Maruts, which was frankly tendentious, proved to be but short-lived. Rudra's affinity with Agni, on the other hand, is perhaps more constant and enduring. Indeed, in some passages, Rudra seems to have been actually identified with Agni (II. 1. 6; *AV* VII. 87. 1; *TS* V. 4. 3. 1). But this is obviously just incidental and not essential. The word *rudra* was primarily used as an epithet of Agni, but later it tended to be interpreted as another name of Agni. It is also natural that the terrific form (*ghorā tanūḥ*) of Agni should be referred to as *rudra* (*TS* II. 2. 2. 3). The legend that, on a certain occasion, Agni wept and was, therefore, called Rudra (*TS* II. 2. 10)[^268_157] is patently an afterthought. On the contrary, it is not improbable that, since, on account of his peculiar character and associations, Rudra's name was taboo among the Vedic poet-priests, he was often represented as concealing under the names of Agni.[^268_158] And, if Rudra was really identical with Agni, how could one explain Rudra's peculiar position in the hieratic Vedic ritual?[^268_159] As against this, the points of [[P269]] similarity between Rudra on the one hand and Śarva and Bhava (*VS* 16. 18, 28), Paśu-pati (frequently in *VS* and *AV*: RGB connects this name—not convincingly—with the Ṛgvedic epithet *paśupā*), Tryambaka (*VS* 3. 58), and post-Vedic Śiva are particularly vital and intrinsic.

But it is Rudra's unmistakable connection with certain religious cults of ancient times which throws a very suggestive light on the essential personality and character of that god. The Keśin hymn (X. 136), for instance, relates to the orgiastic cult of the Munis who are said to have worn long hair, indulged in a sort of ecstasy-producing potion, and attained certain miraculous powers which were comparable to those attained through Yoga; and, in that hymn, Rudra is represented almost as the leader of that cult (X. 136. 7). The cult of the Brahma-cārins is glorified in *AV* XI. 5.[^269_160] These Brahma-cārins practised severe penance and thereby became endowed with peculiar virile powers which enabled them to undertake even cosmic activities. The description of the Brahma-cārin tends to remind one of Vedic Rudra. Similar is the case of the Vrātyas (*AV* XV) who had developed their own distinctive religious ideology, social order, and general way of life. Vedic Rudra betrays many points of contact with their supreme god whom they called Eka-vrātya.[^269_160a]

Among the features of the Vedic Rudra which differentiate him from the major Vedic gods must also be mentioned his association with what may be broadly called the demon-world, with the serpents, and, as indicated above, generally with non-social, non-divine, fear-inspiring elements. It is, however, not correct to conclude on the strength of such references, as RGB does, that "the sentiment of fear is at the bottom" of the worship of Rudra-Śiva.[^269_161]

### Storm-god vs. god of death

The most common theory regarding the character of Rudra is that he is the roaring storm-god who is both frightful and beneficent. RGB has endorsed this theory without any discussion, but specifying that Rudra represented the destructive agency of lightning. According to him, the simple folk working in the field saw in the black cloud tinged red by the soft glow of the lightning god Rudra with his blue neck and red complexion.[^270_162] In the present context, it is neither possible nor necessary to subject this theory to any critical examination. Suffice it to say with OLDENBERG that, in the Vedic descriptions of Rudra, lightnings do not flash, rains do not pour down, winds do not rush forth.[^270_163] Therefore, at least in the consciousness of the Vedic poets, Rudra was not a god of tempest.[^270_164] Moreover that theory miserably fails to explain, rationally and satisfactorily, the complex personality of Rudra with its distinctive and exclusive features as set forth above. As for Rudra's identification with Agni, it has already been shown to be just incidental and not essential.[^270_165] According to OLDENBERG,[^270_166] who considers Rudra's connection with mountains and forests to be far more vital and essential than that with storm and lightning, characterises that god as the Old Man of the Mountains, a demon of disease coming from forest and mountain, like Mars Silvanus. But, while emphasizing one significant aspect of Rudra's character,[^270_167] OLDENBERG has completely ignored other equally or [[P271]] even more important aspects. ARBMAN[^271_168], like OLDENBERG, rejects the theory that Rudra is the god of thunderstorm or that he personifies any other phenomenon of nature. According to him, Rudra is an unheavenly product of lower mythology, an earthly, demonic, fearful god who has originated from the primitive concepts of death and its fears. He further asserts that Ṛgvedic Rudra represents an artificial side-growth of the continuous line of development from the original primitive non-Vedic Rudra to the later Vedic Rudra, and then to the epic Śiva.

See: "Asura Varuṇa" and "New light on the Vedic god Savitṛ" in *Vedic Mythological Tracts*.

There is no doubt that in the Vedic literature, particularly in the *RV*, greater emphasis is put on Rudra's association with death and its horror than on any other aspects of his complex personality and character. Rudra's *sāyakas* (II. 33. 10) are the death-bringing arrows shot by Rudra, the sender of disease and death. In I. 114. 10, Rudra's missiles are unequivocally characterised as 'cow-slaying' and 'man-slaying.' In IV. 3. 6, that god is directly called *nṛhan*, the slayer of men. A reference has already been made to the significance of the relationship which was tendentiously established between Rudra and Maruts (who originally represented the spirits of the dead), after that god had been admitted to the Vedic pantheon.[^271_169] Rudra's character as the god of death is further concretised, so to say, in later Veda. In the *AV*, for instance, he is very significantly described as assailing his [[vicitms|victims]] with diseases (XI. 2. 22, 26; VI. 90; 93). Rudra's doubles, Bhava and [[Sarva|Śarva]], also are addressed in that Veda as gods of death (*AV* XI. 2. 2). Various details of the Vedic [[P272]] ritual in which Rudra figures confirm his character as god of death. In the Sākamedha, for instance, there is a funeral ceremony which includes an offering to Rudra. Incidentally it may be pointed out here that, according to the primitive belief, the god of death and his hosts also bestowed fertility on land, animals, and human beings. It also needs to be added that, since the entire concept relating to Rudra was foreign to the Vedic ideology, the character of that god was not clearly defined in the Veda, and the emphasis often shifted from one aspect of his character to another. That is why Rudra cannot be said to have been represented in the Veda as god of death either exclusively or consistently.

[^271_169]: When later, in the evolutionary Vedic mythology, Yama came to be regarded as the regular god of death, the relationship between Rudra and Maruts was brought to an end and Maruts began to be represented as the hosts of Indra.

The following points will now have been clear from the foregoing discussion: (1) that Rudra was essentially different from the normal hieratic Vedic gods; (2) that he belonged to a *milieu* which was foreign to the Vedic poet-priests; (3) that the Vedic poet-priests must have accommodated him in their religious complex under the pressure of circumstances, and, therefore, reluctantly and almost under protest; (4) that, while doing so, they either suppressed or transformed many of that god's essential traits; and (5) that, as a result of this, only one aspect of Rudra's original complex character, namely, that of the god of death came to be generally emphasized in the Vedic literature. One does, however, get a few glimpses of Rudra's original complex character from various stray references to that god in the Veda. Indeed, some indications are available in the Vedic literature itself of the rise of Rudra, in the form of Rudra-Śiva, to the position of supreme godhead. For instance, various references in *AV* XV suggest Rudra's correspondence with Eka-vrātya (XV. 1) and with Bhava, Śarva, Paśu-pati, Ugra Deva, Mahā-deva, and Īśāna (XV. 5). And, already in the *Maitrāyaṇī-Saṃhitā* II. 9. 1, Rudra is identified with Puruṣa and Mahā-deva.

## Proto-Indian Śiva-Paśupati

There is sufficient evidence to justify the assumption that Rudra is but the Vedic version of a well-established pre-Vedic non-Aryan popular god. The religious cult centering round this proto-Indian god had spread far and wide — different aspects of [[P273]] that god and different forms of his worship having been emphasized in different regions of the country. The essential common basis of that religion is, however, quite unmistakable. This popular proto-Indian religion differed from the Vedic religion in many significant respects. For instance, that religion was iconic, that is to say, the followers of that religion worshipped their god in the form of some concrete symbol which was installed either in the open or within some temple-like structures which were usually erected away from the main settlements. The Vedic religion, on the other hand, generally did not know of any temple or image-worship. Perhaps the earliest suggestive literary reference to idols is to be found in Pāṇini V. 2. 101 (*arcā*: also *pratikṛti* in V. 3. 96: also cf. V. 3. 99). Idol-worship, accordingly, seems to have come into vogue among the followers of Brahmanism not very long before Pāṇini's time. It may be suggested that, with the beginning of the movement for revitalising and popularising Brahmanism which [[crystalised|crystallised]] in the Sūtra-Vedāṅga period, several elements of the popular proto-Indian religion came to be assimilated into the Brahmanic religion, idol-worship being perhaps one of such elements. The proto-Indian worship consisted mainly of *pūjā*, that is besmearing the icon of the god with some sacred substance, and Bali, that is, the offering, made directly to the god, of raw flesh and blood. This mode of worship thoroughly contrasted with the Vedic ritual or *homa* in which usually cooked oblations (or Soma) were offered to the gods, not directly but through the sacrificial fire, in the accompaniment of Mantras. Like idol-worship, the word *pūjā* and also the religious practice denoted by that word were adopted by Brahmanism during the Sūtra-Vedāṅga period — and that too presumably for the very reason for which idol-worship was adopted. Some of the other features of the proto-Indian religion which clearly differentiated it from the Vedic religion were Yoga, penance and austerities, phallic cult, the god's lordship over animals and association with serpents, fertility rites, importance given to Mother Goddess and to female divinities, and the propitiation of the spirits of the dead and their leader. To anticipate, most of these features are present, in one form or another, in the religious ideology relating to Vedic Rudra.

### The "red god" and *pūjā*

The proto-Indian god was celebrated as 'red god' in some circles, particularly among the proto-Dravidians. They called him by the name Śiva (which proto-Dravidian word means the 'red one'), because they worshipped him, among other things, by pouring over (Tamil *pūcu* from which the word *pūjā* is derived) his icon the blood of the animal, more particularly the buffalo (or, in some cases, even the human being), which was offered to him, and, as a consequence of this, the god actually looked red. Vedic Rudra has borrowed not only the name but also some characteristics of the red god. The word, *rudra*, has to be derived from a hypothetical lost root, *rud*, which must have meant 'to be ruddy or red'.[^274_170] Thus, Rudra, like proto-Dravidian Śiva, also means the red god. As pointed out earlier, Rudra is actually associated with the red colour in a variety of ways. He is described as being *aruṣa* (I. 114. 5), *babhru* (II. 33. 5), *tāmra*, *aruṇa*, *vilohita*, *nīla-lohita* (*TS*. IV. 5. 1; *VS* 16. 6, 7). Like the proto-Indian Śiva, Rudra also is offered Bali (and not *homa*) as in the Śūla-gava rite. Rudra's fondness for blood is frequently referred to. The *TS* (II. 1. 7. 1 ff.), for instance, narrates the legend of *vaṣaṭ-kāra* breaking open the head of *gāyatrī*. On that occasion, we are told, the gods took the various juices issuing out of that head. But Rudra chose only the blood. The *ĀpŚS* (X. 13. 11) seems to refer to blood as Rudra's water. In the Aśva-medha, the blood of the horse is assigned to Rudra (*TS* I. 4. 36), and, according to the *Śata-patha-Brāhmaṇa* (V. 3. 1. 10), Rudra longs for the cows slaughtered in the royal palace in the Rāja-sūya. Red colour is also suggestive of death, magic, evil omen, swearing, cursing, misfortune, etc. And it can be shown that both proto-Dravidian Śiva and Vedic Rudra were intimately connected with these concepts implied by the red colour.

[^274_170]: Cf. *ZDMG* 40, 120. The word *rudra* is derived from various roots: *rud* (to howl or to crackle); *ru* (to roar) and *dru* (to speed forth); *rud* (to shine); *ruj* (to shatter). Sāyaṇa, in his commentary on *ṚV* I. 114. 1 explains the word in six different ways. All these etymologies are linguistically unsound.

When the Vedic poet-priests felt constrained by the force of circumstances to admit the popular proto-Indian red god to [[P275]] their pantheon, they naturally sought either to suppress or to transform many of his characteristic features. For instance, they retained the name of the god, but in a different form. They called him Rudra instead of Śiva. As has been pointed out earlier, it was the frightful, demonic aspect of Rudra's character that was specifically stressed in the Vedic literature. This fact made his name almost a taboo. The Vedic poet-priests tried to get over this contingency by referring to that god euphemistically.[^275_171] They gave him the epithet *śiva*, which in Sanskrit meant 'gracious', 'benevolent'.[^275_172] Several other Vedic gods also were characterised as *śiva*, but it was only in the case of Rudra that the epithet became, in course of time, the proper name of the god. It is not unlikely that this happened under the influence of what may be called folk-philology. For, the word *śiva* was phonetically similar to the name of the proto-Dravidian god (who was the direct ancestor of Vedic Rudra) but which (in Sanskrit) denoted just the opposite of what Rudra generally represented in the Veda. The name Śiva as such seems to be unknown to the *Saṃhitās* (except perhaps in *ṚV* VII. 18. 7 where Śiva seems to be the name of a tribe), the *Brāhmaṇas*, and the old Buddhist literature. It is only in the *Śvetāśvatara-Upaniṣad* that the word is first used as a proper noun. Thereafter both Rudra and Śiva began to be used as the names of one and the same god. Incidentally it may be added that Pāṇini mentions Śiva as a proper noun (IV. 1. 112), but he does not mention god Śiva by the side of Indra, Varuṇa, Bhava, Śarva, and Rudra (IV. 1. 49).

[^275_171]: In this context, a reference may be made to the fact that for a similar reason, Erinyes or Greek Furies was renamed as Eumenides. Adoption of a euphemistic name represents an attempt to civilize a terrible deity.

[^275_172]: It may, however, be pointed out that in the *ṚV* the epithet *śiva* is not used exclusively with reference to Rudra. Indeed, *ṚV* X. 92. 9 is the only passage where Rudra is characterised as *śiva*. The epithet *śiva* becomes peculiar to Rudra in the *YV*. It seems that Rudrāṇī also is euphemistically represented as Umā.

### Indus proto-Śiva

There are indications that the Indus valley religion was related to the proto-Dravidian Śiva-religion: both were iconic; both sponsored phallus-worship; the buffalo played a special role [[P276]] in both; both served as the fountain-head of the religion of epic Śiva. In the pre-Vedic Indus valley civilization, the proto-Indian god was represented as ithyphallic Yogīśvara Paśu-pati. The famous Mohenjodaro seal,[^276_173] for instance, shows "the God, who is three-faced, is seated on a low Indian throne in a typical attitude of yoga with legs bent double beneath him, heel to heel, and toes turned downwards. His arms are outstretched, his hands, with thumbs to front resting on his knees...The lower limbs are bare and the phallus seemingly exposed...Crowning his head is a pair of horns meeting in a tall head-dress. To either side of the God are four animals, an elephant and tiger on his proper right, a rhinoceros and buffalo on his left. Beneath the throne are two deer standing with heads regardant and horns turned to the centre...."[^276_174] It is needless to add that all the typical features of this god are evidenced in the conception of Vedic Rudra and epic Śiva.[^276_175] As has been already pointed out, Rudra is connected with Yoga through his association with the Muni-cult while epic Śiva is often glorified as yogīśvara. The hieratic Vedic attitude towards Yoga and related practices, like renunciation, austerities, and wandering mendicancy, is reflected in Indra's antagonism to the Yatis (*TS* 6. 2. 7. 5; *Ait. Br.* VII. 28). The Indus valley religion, like the classical Śiva-religion, promoted the worship of the anthropomorphic ithyphallic god as well as of the separate phallic symbol. The description of some of the Vrātyas as *śamaṇīcameḍhra* and of the Brahma-cārin as *bṛhac-chepa* tends to establish Vedic Rudra (who was associated with these cults) as representing a significant stage in the almost unbroken continuity from the Indus valley Paśu-pati to epic Śiva.[^276_176] As in the case of [[P277]] Yoga, so too in the case of phallic worship, the attitude of Vedism was contentious. The *śiśna-devas* (phallus-worshippers) were regarded as the enemies of the Vedic Aryans.[^277_177]

[^276_173]: Marshall, *Mohenjodaro and the Indus Civilization* (*MIC*) I, pl. xii, fig. 17.

[^276_174]: *Op. cit.*, pp. 52 ff.

[^276_175]: Some scholars do not accept the suggestion that the Mohenjodaro God is the direct precursor of epic Śiva.

[^276_176]: A. Ghosh (*IC* 2, 763–71) thinks that Vedic Rudra is a storm-god; and yet he links him up with the Indus valley god on the strength of the evidence of the ellipsoid stones, which, in the context of Rudra, are to be regarded as thunderbolt stones. This is obviously not convincing.

[^277_177]: Cf. *ṚV* VII. 21. 5; X. 99. 3.

The Indus valley god's lordship over animals is clearly suggested by the Mohenjodaro seal. Vedic Rudra (as also epic Śiva) is actually called Paśu-pati (*VS* 16. 17, 40), while most of the animals shown on the Mohenjodaro seal are brought into relation with epic Śiva in a special manner.[^277_178] The Indus valley Paśupati's tall head-dress perhaps reappears in the form of Rudra's *uṣṇīṣa* (*VS* 16. 22) and *kaparda* (*VS* 16. 43). And though, neither Vedic Rudra nor epic Śiva is represented with horns, there is hardly any doubt that the horns do reappear in the form of the crescent moon which Śiva bears on his forehead.[^277_179] Another Indus valley seal[^277_180] provides evidence for the assumption that the serpent cult was an integral part of the Indus valley Śiva-religion. Vedic Rudra also is associated with serpents in various [[P278]] ways. For instance, *AV* III. 27 and VI. 56. 2–3, which are said to pertain to Rudra, are regarded as serpent charms.[^278_181]

[^277_178]: Śiva is described as wearing the skin of an elephant (*Kumāra.* 5. 67; *Megha. Pūrva*, 38). Communal offerings of buffalos were made to the proto-Dravidian Śiva. The buffalo, as the vehicle of the god of death, may also have been originally connected with Śiva. As the "Old Man of the Mountains" and as an archer, Vedic Rudra and epic Śiva are closely connected with wild animals like rhinoceros and tiger (cf. *AV* XI. 2. 24: *āraṇyāḥ paśavaḥ* mentioned in connection with Rudra). Śiva is referred to as *hariṇeśa* and *mṛga-rūpa* in a Stotra occurring in the Himavat-khaṇḍa of the *Skanda-Purāṇa*. The bull must have been regarded as the vehicle of the Indus valley god (as of the epic Śiva), and, as it often happens, some special feature of the vehicle is transferred to the god himself and sometimes also to his worshippers. Apart from the proto-Dravidian Śiva and the Indus valley Paśu-pati (whose identity will have now become quite manifest), two other forms of the same proto-Indian god are referred to in the *Śata-patha-Brāhmaṇa* (I. 7. 3. 8) – Śarva[^278_182] who was glorified by the Eastern people and Bhava who was glorified by the Vāhīkas.[^278_183] It is significant that both these regions lay outside the brahmanised country.

[^278_182]: According to MACHEK (*Arch. Or.* 22, 544–62), Śarva is the original name of Rudra, the latter being merely an epithet. Thus Rudra is not a storm-god as RGB supposes, but represents just the Vedic version of the proto-Indian Śiva-Paśu-pati. It is to this latter god, and not to Vedic Rudra (as RGB says), that the origin of Śaivism is to be traced back. Indeed, most of the essential features of Śaivism are anticipated by the proto-Indian religion of Śiva-Paśu-pati and consequently by the religious complex pertaining to Vedic Rudra.[^278_184]

## *Brāhmaṇas*, Pāṇini, and Kauṭilya

I have suggested[^278_185] that the word *śivāsaḥ* occurring in *ṚV* VII. 18. 7 may denote the people who worshipped Śiva as their chief god.[^278_186] The passage mentions various tribes who tried to [[P279]] win over Indra to their side – of course, hypocritically – against the Vedic Aryan leader Sudās in the Dāśarājña. The Viṣāṇins, that is, the people wearing a head-dress adorned with horns (presumably, like their god depicted on the Mohenjodaro seal) are mentioned side by side with the Śivas. This may be understood as confirming the character of the Śivas as the worshippers of Śiva. It would appear that the Śivas, the Viṣāṇins, and the Śiśna-devas, who are represented in the *ṚV* as the enemies of the Vedic Aryans, professed the same proto-Indian Śiva-religion. Apart from this solitary and not improbable reference, the word *śiva* does not occur in the *Saṃhitās* or the *Brāhmaṇas* in the sense of either god Śiva or the worshipper of Śiva. As pointed out by RGB,[^279_187] in the *Śata-patha-Brāhmaṇa* VI. 1. 3. 7 and the *Kauṣītaki-Brāhmaṇa* VI. 1, 9, Rudra is referred to as the son of Uṣas. Prajā-pati is said to have given him eight names, seven of which are the same as those mentioned in *AV* XV. 5. 1–7 while the eighth is Aśani. More significant is the reference to Rudra roving from place to place, hankering after the sacrificer's cattle and then being propitiated by an offering on the cross-road (*Śata-patha-Brāhmaṇa* II. 6. 2. 6). It may also be noted that the cult of the Vrātyas receives special attention in the *Pañcaviṃśa-Brāhmaṇa*, while the reference to Muni Aitaśa in the state of ecstasy (*Ait. Br.* VI. 5. 7) and to Keśin Dārbhya (*Kauṣ. Br.* VII. 4) remind one of the Muni cult. All this does not, however, imply any particular development in the Rudra-Śiva religion as reflected in the *Brāhmaṇas*. To suggest, as WEBER does,[^279_188] on the strength of the *Kauṣītaki-Brāhmaṇa* ascribing the epithets *īśāna* and *mahān deva* to Rudra, that the beginnings of the sectarian Rudra-worship are thereby indicated does not seem to be warranted.[^279_189] Nor is sufficient evidence available in the *Brāhmaṇas* to justify KEITH'S observation[^279_190] that [[P280]] in the period of the *Brāhmaṇas* the old polytheism was declining and Śaivism was gaining ground.

## *Śvetāśvatara-Upaniṣad*

The *Śvetāśvatara-Upaniṣad* (*ŚU*) represents a very significant landmark in the history of Śaivism. That *Upaniṣad* is in respect of Śaivism what the *Bhagavad-gītā* is in respect of Vāsudevism-Kṛṣṇaism. There is, however, no doubt that the *Bhagavad-gītā* is chronologically later than the *ŚU*. It can, indeed, be shown that many concepts occurring in the *ŚU* have been further developed in the *Bhagavad-gītā*.[^281_198] The *ŚU* (V. 2) mentions Kapila, the founder of the Sāṃkhya system,[^281_199] who may be taken to have belonged to the 6th century B.C. That would fix the terminus ante quem non for the origin of that *Upaniṣad*. Further, from the grammatical point of view, that *Upaniṣad* is clearly post-Pāṇinian.[^281_200] The *ŚU* may accordingly be assigned to about the end of the 5th century B.C. Conceptually, the *ŚU* may be placed between the *Kaṭha-Up.* and the *Bhagavad-gītā*.[^281_201]

[^281_198]: For instance the concept of *uttama puruṣa* in the *BG* (XV. 17) is presumably developed out of the concept of *mahān puruṣa* in the *ŚU* (III. 8, 19).

[^281_199]: Max Mueller (SBE XV, p. 255), among others, understands the word *kapila*, in this context, to mean 'fiery' or 'ruddy'. Deussen identifies Kapila or 'the red savant' with Hiraṇya-garbha.

[^281_200]: Cf. Kirfel, *Beiträge zur Geschichte der Nominalkomposition in den Upaniṣads und im Epos*, Bonn 1908; Wecker, "Der Gebrauch der Kasus in der älteren Upaniṣad-Literatur"; Fuerst, "Der Sprachgebrauch der älteren Upaniṣads", *KZ* 47, pp. 1 ff. It is also suggested that the form *brahman* occurring in the *ŚU* I. 12 betrays the influence of Prakrits.

[^281_201]: The *ŚU* belongs to what Deussen has called the "middle group" of the *Upaniṣads*. From the lexical point of view, Jacobi supports this view (*Die Entwicklung der Gottesidee bei den Indern*, pp. 20 ff.).

### Monotheism and Sāṃkhya-Yoga

The *ŚU* is essentially monotheistic in character. *Eko devaḥ*[^281_202] – these words may, indeed, be said to be the key-words of this philosophical tract. This One God is beyond all contingency and variation, and yet he is the creator as well as the destroyer – the Urgrund – of the world. He also sustains everything: *bharate viśvam īśaḥ* (I. 8). It is verily through his greatness that this *brahmacakra* is set in motion (VI. 1). He is described as being immanent (I. 16; II. 16) as well as transcendent (III. 10, 14) – a kind of polarity which is pregnantly expressed and which deeply touches the heart.[^281_203] And, manifestly, this *eka deva* is Rudra:

[^281_202]: Cf. I. 10; III. 3; etc.

[^281_203]: Cf. J. W. Hauer, *ZDMG* 84, p. \*99\*.

[[P282]] *eka eva rudro 'vatasthe na dvitīyo ya imāṃl lokān īśata īśanībhiḥ* (III. 2).[^282_204] He is now definitely identified with Śiva (IV. 14, 15, 18), and is also referred to as Hara (I. 10), Maheśvara (VI. 7), Bhagavat (V. 4), Giriśanta – Giritra (III. 5, 6), and Mahān Puruṣa (III. 8). His description as *vṛkṣa iva stabdhaḥ* (III. 9) seems to suggest his name Sthāṇu. In its glorification of Rudra-Śiva as the One God, the *ŚU* is evidently influenced by the concept of Eka-vrātya (*AV* XV) and the descriptions in the *Śata-rudrīya*.[^282_205] Verily, the *ŚU* represents a kind of philosophical continuation of the ancient *Śata-rudrīya*.

[^282_204]: This is the reading accepted by R. Hauschild in his edition of the *ŚU* (Leipzig 1927: Reprint, 1966).

[^282_205]: *AV* X. 8 must also be mentioned in this connection.

Rudra being thus represented in the *ŚU* as the Supreme Being, it is difficult to agree with RGB's observation that this *Upaniṣad* "does not appear to be composed in a sectarian spirit".[^282_206] Again, Rudra in the *ŚU* is not, as RGB seems to suggest,[^282_207] the result of the personalisation of the impersonal Brahman. There are clear indications in the *Upaniṣad* that the God is actually superior to Brahman – he is *brahmayoni* (V. 6) and *brahmapara* (III. 7). It is true that Sāṃkhya (VI. 13), Yoga (VI. 13), and Vedānta (VI. 22) – all these three are mentioned by name in the *ŚU*, but the teachings of that *Upaniṣad* are basically Sāṃkhya-Yoga-oriented,[^282_208] the Vedāntic elements in [[P283]] them, if any, being only superficial.[^283_209] The word *māyā* occurring in this *Up.* is, for instance, to be understood not in the strict Śāṃkara-Vedāntic sense of illusion, but rather in the Ṛgvedic sense of the *māyā* of Asura Rudra – the *devātma-śakti* and its creation. The *Upaniṣad* thus seeks to invest life with some meaning and value as it were. In the ultimate analysis, however, the general tendency of the *Upaniṣad* is, like that of the *Bhagavad-gītā*, to bring about a kind of philosophical synthesis.

[^282_206]: *Op. cit.*, p. 151. Hauer (*Op. cit.*) even suggests that Śvetāśvatara may have been a name of Rudra-Śiva. Incidentally, the *ŚU* is referred to in the commentary as the *Mantropaniṣad* of the Śvetāśvataras. Traditionally, the Śvetāśvatara is regarded as a branch of the Taittirīya school of *KYV*. The commentary, however, speaks of *ātharvaṇikāḥ śvetāśvatarāḥ*.

[^282_207]: *Op. cit.*, pp. 151–52. Macnicol (*Indian Theism*) characterises Rudra in the *ŚU* as "a deity of agnosticism."

[^282_208]: Cf. E. H. Johnston, "Some Sāṃkhya and Yoga conceptions of the *ŚU*", *JRAS* 1930, 855–878. Johnston, indeed, regards the *ŚU* as "the oldest document we have giving an adequate account of Sāṃkhya views". In the paper mentioned here, he discusses the *ŚU* I. 4–5, for the explanation of which, according to him, one has to look to the Sāṃkhya system. He concludes that "the author of the Upaniṣad was fully acquainted with the Sāṃkhya conceptions of *prakṛti*, its evolutes and subordinate categories, but in a form more primitive than that of the *SK* (*Sāṃkhya-Kārikā*)." Johnston further discusses in that paper the *ŚU* II. 8–15 (particularly II. 10) which refer to Yoga practices. The *ŚU* is also regarded (A. G. Krishna Warrier, *ALB* 17, p. 96) as the earliest authentic source of our knowledge of Yoga. The *Up.* contains many technical terms from Sāṃkhya-Yoga (I. 8, 10; IV. 5). Strangely enough, Deussen thinks (*Sechzig Upaniṣad's des Veda*, 1921, p. 301) that Sāṃkhya and Yoga in the *ŚU* are to be understood not as systems, but in the sense of *vaidikaṃ jñānaṃ dhyānaṃ ca*.

### Bhakti and grace

Mokṣa is naturally the goal according to the *ŚU*, and it is to be attained by means of the knowledge of the One God: *jñātvā devaṃ mucyate sarva-pāśaiḥ* (II. 15). The *Upaniṣad* does not seem to tire of repeating this dictum (IV. 14–15). But far more important in this connection is Bhakti for god Rudra-Śiva. Indeed, as has been rightly observed, the most characteristic feature of the religious sects of India, in general, is that they subordinate knowledge to a psychical fact of a totally different nature, namely, Bhakti, that is, faith, humble submission, absolute devotion, love for God, without which knowledge is either vain or impossible.[^283_209a] It has been already pointed out that the cult of Bhakti, which was presumably pre-Vedic and non-Aryan in origin, [[P284]] must have come to be revived some time before Pāṇini's time.[^284_210] It is but natural that the Bhakti-cult should have been specifically related, first of all, to the god who had been known to the people from very early times, namely, Rudra-Śiva. As required by the doctrine of Bhakti, the worship of Rudra-Śiva was open to all people irrespective of race, *varṇa*, or *āśrama*.[^284_210a] The *ŚU* emphasizes that it is through meditation on,[^284_211] application to, and identification with Hara that the unreality, that the world is, finally comes to an end: *tasyā 'bhidhyānād yojanāt tattva-bhāvād bhūyaś cā 'nte viśva-māyā-nivṛttiḥ* (I. 10). And even the fact that Śvetāśvatara is enabled to impart his esoteric teaching to a person who has the deepest Bhakti for the God and for the Guru[^284_212] is itself the result of his *tapas* but more particularly of the grace of God (VI. 21, 23).[^284_213]

[^283_209a]: Cf. Barth, *Op. cit.*, p. 218. Also: Renou, *Religions of Ancient India*, p. 93.

[^284_210]: See *supra*.

[^284_210a]: The word *atyāśramin* used in the *ŚU* (6. 21) is significant. It would remind one of the description of the Vrātyas in the *Pañcaviṃśa-Brāhmaṇa*, e.g. *na brahma-caryaṃ caranti*.

[^284_211]: The knowledge of the God, mentioned above, is actually included in meditation.

[^284_212]: The Guru-tradition is peculiar to the Śiva-religion as is evidenced by the *Brahma-cāri-sūkta* in the *AV*. See *infra*.

[^284_213]: *Prasāda* or grace of god is an essential element of the Bhakti doctrine.

### Later Śaiva *Upaniṣads*

A few other *Upaniṣads* are characterised as Śaiva,[^284_214] but most of them belong to a late date. A brief reference may, however, be made here to some significant points from a couple of these *Upaniṣads*. The *Atharva-śira-Up.*,[^284_215] which has some verses common with the *ŚU*, opens with the gods' query regarding the nature of Rudra and Rudra's definite assertion that he is the one eternal god and that none besides him exists. Among Rudra's various names mentioned in this *Upaniṣad*, attention may be specially drawn to Bhagavat and Mahā-deva (44). The *Upaniṣad* also speaks of the Pāśupata-vrata (67), the *bhasma-dhāraṇa-vidhi* (which is a typically Śaiva practice) (67), and the *paśu-pāśa-vimokṣa* (68).

[^284_214]: The Adyar Library Series No. 9, 1950, contains 14 such texts besides the *ŚU*.

[^284_215]: RGB mentions only this one.

[[P285]] The *Kaivalya-Up.* (2. 5) glorifies the study of the *Śata-rudriya* as the source of true philosophical knowledge: *yaḥ śata-rudriyam adhīte so 'gnipūto bhavati... anena jñānam āpnoti saṃsārārṇava-nāśanam*. Similarly, the *Jābāla-Up.*[^285_216] (3) declares that the murmuring of the *Śata-rudriya* leads to *amṛtatva*. The concluding portion of the *Pañca-brahma-Up.* characterises Śiva as *sac-cid-ānanda* and *saṃsāra-mocaka*. The *Maitrī-Up.*, which, strictly speaking, is not a Śaiva *Upaniṣad*, is nonetheless important in that it seems to refer to different Śaiva sects and their practices (VII. 8). The *Kena-Up.*, which presents Umā Haimavatī (consort of Rudra-Śiva) as explaining to Indra the mystery (*yakṣa*) that is Brahman (III. 12; IV. 1), may also be incidentally mentioned in the present context.[^285_217]

## Śiva in the *Mahābhārata*

It is particularly noteworthy that, though the *Mahā-bhārata* (*MBh.*) is generally conceived in the interest of Vaiṣṇavism (Vāsudevism-Kṛṣṇaism), Kṛṣṇa being represented almost as the *primo motore* of the Great Epic, the cultus which is often quite emphatically glorified in the epic is that of Śiva. One would, therefore, be justified in assuming that, long before the *MBh.* assumed its final form, the Śiva-cult had re-established itself as a dominant force in the religious history of India. The two major characters of the epic, namely, Kṛṣṇa and Arjuna, are represented to have been the special proteges of Śiva. They are shown to have survived unscathed the horrendous onslaught which Aśvatthāman had mounted against the Pāṇḍava army after the death of his father. When asked by Aśvatthāman how that could have happened, Vyāsa narrated to him how formerly Nārāyaṇa had practised [[panance|penance]] on the Maināka mountain for 60,000 years, how, as the result of it, he had seen Rudra-Śiva appear before him, how he had then praised Śiva as the highest god, and how, finally, Śiva had granted to him the boon of invincibility in battle. [[P286]] The privilege of this favour of Śiva was shared also by Nara who was born of Nārāyaṇa's *tapas*. Nārāyaṇa and Nara, who, in the epoch of the *MBh.*, incarnated themselves as Kṛṣṇa and Arjuna respectively, continued to enjoy that favour of Śiva (Droṇa. 172). A similar explanation was given by Vyāsa in another context. While Arjuna was routing the Kaurava forces, he saw that a resplendent person bearing *śūla* was actually doing so for him. When he reported this to Vyāsa, the latter told him that it was Śiva who had been constantly guarding him throughout the war. Vyāsa then praised Śiva with what is referred to as a *caturvidha stotra* (Droṇa. 173).[^286_218] Verily, as described in the Āraṇyaka-parvan (38–41), Arjuna had obtained his strongest weapon, namely, the *Pāśupata-astra*, from Śiva himself whom Arjuna had propitiated through *tapas* and who, in the guise of a Kirāta, had subsequently put to test Arjuna's fortitude and valour.[^286_219]

[^286_218]: Cf. Droṇa. 173. 103.

[^286_219]: It is suggested (Renou, *Op. cit.*, p. 62) that this struggle of Śiva against Arjuna reflects the opposition which the Śiva-cult met with before it was finally established.

In the first episode mentioned above, a hint was incidentally given by Vyāsa that Aśvatthāman also was in a way cherished by Śiva (Droṇa. 172. 82). This very point seems to have been further developed in the Sauptika-parvan (6–7). When, after having been temporarily dissuaded from his proposed nocturnal raid, Aśvatthāman returned to his camp, he saw a gigantic apparition blocking his way. He attacked that phantom with all his might, but to no avail. He suspected that the phantom was born from his own evil inclinations; he, therefore, surrendered to Śiva, sang him a hymn of praise, and then actually prepared to offer himself as an oblation to the God. Śiva was thereby propitiated. He then told Aśvatthāman of the rigorous austerities which Kṛṣṇa had practised for the sake of his favour and declared that no one was more beloved to him than Kṛṣṇa (Sauptika. 7. 61: *tasmād iṣṭatamaḥ kṛṣṇād anyo mama na vidyate*). Further, in the same parvan, it is narrated that Yudhiṣṭhira asked Kṛṣṇa how Aśvatthāman had, single-handed, dared perpetrate the atrocity [[P287]] during the night and that Kṛṣṇa replied that it was all due to the credulous favour of Śiva. Kṛṣṇa further took that opportunity to expatiate on Śiva's greatness. Brahmā, he continued to tell Yudhiṣṭhira, implored Śiva to create the world and procreate the human race. Śiva agreed and undertook severe penance for that purpose. Having waited for the God for a long time, Brahmā decided to bring about creation through another agency. Śiva returned from his penance and saw that the world had already been created. Greatly enraged, he cut off his generative organ and violently planted it upon the earth, for, he thought, it was no longer needed. Kṛṣṇa also told Yudhiṣṭhira of the sacrifice of the gods in which no portion was offered to Śiva and which, therefore, Śiva had ruthlessly exterminated (Sauptika. 17–18).

### Kṛṣṇa-Upamanyu and *stutis*

But Śiva's unquestioned supremacy is perhaps best illustrated by the Kṛṣṇa-Upamanyu episode described in the Anuśāsana-parvan (14–18). Jāmbuvatī desired a son from Kṛṣṇa. Having known that Rukmiṇī had given birth to valiant sons as the result of Kṛṣṇa's worship of Śiva, she importuned Kṛṣṇa again to worship that God for her sake. Kṛṣṇa went to the Himālaya mountain for penance and casually came to the hermitage of Upamanyu. Upamanyu asked Kṛṣṇa to practise penance there only, for, Śiva himself was residing there. Upamanyu then told Kṛṣṇa how he had practised penance in order to propitiate Śiva, how he had refused to accept boons from any god other than Śiva, and how, in the end, Śiva had manifested himself before him and had fulfilled all his desires (14). Kṛṣṇa, too, is then said to have propitiated Śiva through penance and to have thereby obtained the desired boons (15–16). This narrative is followed by the *Śiva-aṣṭottara-sahasra-nāma-stotra* (17) and the eulogy of that Stotra by different sages (18). In another context, on having been asked by Yudhiṣṭhira what specific knowledge he had obtained from Durvāsas,[^287_220] Kṛṣṇa responded by glorifying the supreme greatness of Rudra-Śiva (Anuśāsana. 145) and by explaining the significance of the great god's various names, such

[^287_220]: It is not unlikely that Durvāsas (mentioned in Anuśāsana. 144) was [[ust|just]] a form of Śiva himself.

[[P288]] as, Maheśvara, Rudra, Mahā-deva, Śiva, Sthāṇu, Paśu-pati, etc. (Anuśāsana. 146). It is noteworthy that, in both these cases, a special mention is made of the *Śata-rudriya* (Anuśāsana. 145. 4; 146. 23).

Various occasions have been manoeuvred by the redactor of the *MBh.* to introduce—appositely or otherwise—hymns of praise of Śiva. Some of the more important of these Śiva-stutis are: by Kṛṣṇa in Droṇa. 172, by Vyāsa in Droṇa. 173, by the gods and the Brahmarṣis on the eve of the destruction of Tripura (Karṇa. 24), by Aśvatthāman in Sauptika. 7, by Upamanyu in Anuśāsana. 14, by Kṛṣṇa in Anuśāsana. 15, and the *Śiva-aṣṭottara-sahasra-nāma-stotra* (Anuśāsana. 17).[^288_221] It may also be mentioned that many *tīrthas* spread over different regions of the country are described in the epic as having been sanctified by association with Śiva, in one form or another—a fact that would indicate the wide sphere of the influence of the Śiva-religion.

[^288_221]: Dakṣa is represented as having praised and propitiated Śiva after the latter had destroyed his sacrifice. This passage (Bom. ed. Śānti. 284) is, however, relegated to the Appendix in the Critical Edition (XII. App. I. 28).

### Epic features of Śaivism

Attention may now be drawn to some characteristic features of the Śiva-religion which become evident from the many allusions to Śiva occurring in the epic. In the epic Śiva-religion, greater importance is attached to *tapas* than to Bhakti or any other form of worship. And this is in full conformity with the proto-Indian character of that religion. Whoever seeks to propitiate Śiva practises penance—for instance, Nārāyaṇa, Upamanyu, and Kṛṣṇa did so. Even Śiva, who is naturally enough represented as the supreme god, himself takes to *tapas* whenever he wants to accomplish something. That phallus worship is an integral part of the Śiva-religion is also frequently emphasized in the epic. Indeed, Upamanyu argues that Śiva is the greatest of the gods because it is only his *liṅga*—and of no one else—that is worshipped by gods (Anuśāsana. 14. 100–102: *na śuśruma yad anyasya liṅgam abhyarcyate suraiḥ*). The suggestion that *liṅga* here means merely a concrete symbol or an indicatory mark [[P289]] would not carry conviction. For, the symbols of even other gods are seen to be actually worshipped. But it is only in the case of Śiva that his generative organ is worshipped. That *liṅga*, in the context of Śiva, invariably signifies the generative organ is confirmed by the legend narrated by Kṛṣṇa to Yudhiṣṭhira regarding Śiva having cut off his *liṅga* when he discovered that it was no longer needed since the process of creation had already been accomplished (Sauptika. 17. 24). Again, the *MBh.* is definitely familiar with the circumstance that Śiva is worshipped in two different forms—in the human form and in the form of *liṅga* (Droṇa. 173. 94: *pūjayed vigrahaṃ yas tu liṅgaṃ vā ’pi samarcayet*; also Anuśāsana. 146. 15–17).

One is often reminded of Śiva's ancient character as the god of fertility in respect of man, animal, and land, when one reads in the epic, not infrequently, of persons propitiating Rudra-Śiva for the sake of progeny (cf. Anuśāsana. 14–16). The almost unbroken continuity of the Śiva-religion from the proto-Indian period to the epic-paurāṇic period is further evidenced, among other things, by Śiva appearing as a Kirāta (Āraṇyaka. 38–41) or as a Puruṣa bearing *śūla* (Droṇa. 173) and by his being denied a share in the sacrifice (Sauptika. 18; Śānti. 274).[^289_222] Similarly, some of the ideological features of Śaivism which were first introduced in the *ŚU* are seen to have been delineated also in the epic—such as, for instance, its Sāṃkhya-Yoga orientation.[^289_223] Supreme godhead of Śiva, which is frequently emphasized in the epic, may also be mentioned in this very context. As for this last, however, it needs to be pointed out that, though Nārāyaṇa-Kṛṣṇa is usually represented as a protege of Śiva, the redactor of the epic, presumably with a view to stressing the Vaiṣṇava genesis of the epic, has introduced some episodes wherein Rudra-Śiva is [[P290]] said to be just a product of the wrath of Nārāyaṇa (Brahmā being the product of his grace)[^290_224] or Nārāyaṇa is shown to have seized Rudra by the throat[^290_225] or Rudra is made to surrender to Nārāyaṇa.[^290_226] But, even in such cases, the redactor either betrays his bias in favour of Śiva (Śānti. 328. 21; *tasmād ātmānam evā ’gre rudraṃ sampūjayāmy aham*) or seeks to establish a kind of equipoise between the two gods (Śānti. 330. 64: *nā ’vayor antaram kiṃcin mā te bhūd buddhir anyathā*).[^290_227]

## *Liṅga* worship

RGB's treatment of phallus-worship is rather inadequate. And this is quite understandable, for, he did not have before him the considerable archaeological material which has become available in recent times. The tradition of phallus-worship in India is, indeed, very ancient. It has been suggested that the Neolithic people in South India were primarily worshippers of *liṅga*. Evidence is produced in this connection of the Kapagallu hill in Bellary district, where one sees a *liṅga* together with a bull in a crouching position.[^290_228] Be that as it may, there is certainly far more definite and far more profuse evidence regarding phallus-worship which has been unearthed in the Indus [[P291]] valley. The conical terracotta objects with rounded tops and the ringstones discovered in the Indus valley are, without doubt, the symbols of *liṅga* and *yoni* respectively. We have already seen that the chief god of the Indus valley people was the ithyphallic Yogīśvara Paśu-pati (who has been depicted on the Mohenjodaro seal and who is identical with the proto-Dravidian god Śiva).[^291_229] The ithyphallicism of the Mohenjodaro figure and the large number of *liṅga-yoni*-like objects discovered in Indus valley may be presumed to have been closely related to each other—verily, they have to be regarded as denoting the two aspects of the phallic worship which was an integral part of the proto-Indian Śiva-religion. It is not unlikely that the worship of the phallus, which, as evidenced by its almost universal dissemination, must have been a natural development in the history of human culture, had grown in India independently in the hoary past,[^291_230] and that it came to be associated with the religion of the ithyphallic god only at some later date. Whatever that may have been, one thing seems to be quite certain, namely, that, in the proto-Indian religion of the Indus valley period, the two had already become organically connected with each other. It is believed that an oblong terracotta sealing discovered in the Indus valley actually shows the figure of Śiva with his three symbols, namely, the bull, the trident, and the phallus.[^291_231] We may, therefore, assume that in the proto-Indian Śiva-religion the god was worshipped both in the anthropomorphic ithyphallic form and in the form of a separate *liṅga*. This practice of the worship of Śiva has continued even in historical times,[^291_232] though [[P292]] the *liṅga-pūjā* seems to have become more common in course of time.

And, secondly, in view of its universal diffusion in very ancient times, the question regarding its origin is rendered almost irrelevant.[^292_235]

### Non-phallic theories critiqued

There has been a tendency among Indian scholars—a tendency which is perhaps quite natural—to reject the assumption that the *liṅga* is a phallic emblem. It is suggested, for instance, that the idea of the *liṅga* originated out of an attempt to express the inexpressible, to make Śiva perceptible to the mental eye.[^292_236] The *liṅga* is Maheśvara himself, and not merely the emblem of his *membrum virile* (*liṅgaṃ sākṣān maheśvaraḥ*).[^292_237] It was also claimed that the *liṅga* signified a protest against idolatry. According to another interpretation, the Śiva-*liṅga* is the closest possible approximation to the cosmic Puruṣa—the hemispherical top of the *liṅga* consists of thousands of heads (*sahasra-śīrṣan*), each of the size of a point. The *liṅga* represents the double idea of the "formless" and the "all-formed" Śiva.[^292_238]

[^292_236]: Cf. V. S. AGRAWALA, *Pur.* 7, 291 ff. The author also says: The *liṅga* form implies the source of creativity that is invisible and unmanifested. The *liṅga* is the concrete manifestation.

[^292_237]: *Liṅga-P.* 20. 15–21; 16. 1.

[^292_238]: The *liṅga* is also believed to symbolise *nāda* supervening the *pīṭha* like a cylinder (cf. SAKHARE, *Op. cit.*, pp. 303 ff.).

[[P293]] But the more common idea which is presumed to underlie the *liṅga* is that of the *axis mundi* (which is also implied by the Atharvavedic Skambha and the Buddhist *stūpa*). Recourse is also taken in this connection to the Purāṇic account of the quarrel for precedence between Brahmā and Viṣṇu and the appearance before them of Śiva in the form of a blazing column the ends of which the two gods were asked to find but which, to their chagrin, they could not. ### Sculptural and literary evidence

The close relationship between the ithyphallic god and the *liṅga-yoni*-like objects, which is clearly implied in the Indus valley religion, should, indeed, put an end to all such speculations. There is also the strong corroborative evidence provided by the Guḍimallam *liṅga* of the second century B.C.[^293_240] whose similarity to the human male's generative organ is quite unmistakable. And the still more pertinent point is that, at Guḍimallam, an anthropomorphic figure of Śiva, in standing position and with two arms, and the *liṅga* appear to be inseparably connected with each other.[^293_241] This would put beyond all doubt three facts, namely, (1) that the Śiva-cult and the *liṅga*-cult organically constituted a single religious complex, (2) that the *liṅga* signified the phallus, and (3) that, as in the proto-Indian period so too in historical times, Śiva was worshipped in two forms—in the human form and/or in the *liṅga*-form. ### Numismatic evidence

In view of what has been said above, RGB's statement that the *liṅga*-worship had not come into use at the time of Patañjali[^295_250] cannot be said to be valid. No doubt Patañjali does not refer to *liṅga*-worship, but that is just because he has no occasion to do so. Similarly RGB's contention that the *liṅga*-cult must have been unknown even in the time of Wema Kadphises (65–75 A.D.), because, while his coins show the figure of Śiva as well as his emblems, the trident and the bull, they do not have the *liṅga* represented on them,[^295_251] cannot bear scrutiny. For, there is the evidence of an uninscribed cast coin of circa second century B.C., which has on its reverse a tree in railing on the left and *liṅga* on a square pedestal on the right.[^295_252] A *liṅga* can be recognised on a pedestal between two different trees in railing on some Ujjain [[P296]] coins of the third-second century B.C. It is also found on one or two copper coins from Taxila of about the same period.[^296_253] Probably, there is a *liṅga* on a square pedestal in the centre of a punch-marked coin.[^296_254]

## Patañjali's *Mahābhāṣya*

The few references to the Śiva-cult which we come across in the *Mahā-bhāṣya* of Patañjali clearly indicate that that cult had become firmly established long before his time. Commenting on P. V. 3.99,[^296_255] Patañjali seems to suggest that, even in the fourth century B.C., the Śiva-worship had become so common that the Mauryas got the images of gods like Śiva, Skanda, and Viśākha[^296_256] prepared and sold to the people. In another context, Patañjali apparently hints that Śiva did not formally belong to the hierarchical Vedic pantheon. In his commentary on Vārttika 2 of P. VI. 3. 26,[^296_257] he specifically mentions the *dvandva* compounds formed with the names of the gods Brahmā and Prajā-pati, Śiva and Vaiśravaṇa,[^296_258] and Skanda and Viśākha as not being covered by the category of what he calls *loka-vedayoḥ devatā-dvandva*. But perhaps the most significant reference in the *Mahā-bhāṣya*, from the point of view of the history of the Śiva-religion, is that to the Śiva-bhāgavatas.[^296_259] The Śiva-bhāgavatas must have constituted a specific sect of the followers of the Śiva-religion — the distinctive characteristics of that sect being Bhakti for Śiva[^296_260] and the bearing of an iron lance (trident) in the hand. According to [[P297]] Patañjali's commentary on P. V. 2. 76, a Śiva-bhāgavata cannot be called *āyaḥśūlika* though he carries an *ayaḥ-śūla* in his hand. The word *āyaḥśūlika* is not to be understood in the literal sense, but only in the metaphorical sense. It denotes a person who takes recourse to harsh measures in order to accomplish some work which can be accomplished by milder methods. The context seems to suggest that the *āyaḥśūlika* was a kind of extortionist false ascetic, like the *daṇḍājinika*, but his connection with the Śiva-religion, if any, is not clear.

## Coins, inscriptions, and Hiuen-Tsang

RGB's exploitation of the epigraphic material for the reconstruction of the history of Śaivism is, as usual, perfect. The numismatic evidence for the prevalence of the Śiva-religion, particularly in the first couple of centuries before and after Christ, is also not inconsiderable. It is noteworthy that most of this evidence is derived from north-western India. For instance, a bronze seal, discovered by MARSHALL at Taxila and belonging to the first century B.C., shows the figure of Śiva and the legend *śivarakṣitasa* in Brāhmī and Kharoṣṭhī characters.[^297_261] The coins of the Ārjunāyanas (cir. 50 B.C.) and the Yaudheyas (cir. 150 A.D.) show the bull which is obviously a Śaiva emblem. The Audumbaras (end of the first century B.C.) issued their coins in the name of the god Bhagavat Mahā-deva. A reference has already been made to the coins of Wema Kadphises who called himself Māheśvara. One of the prominent Śaiva sects was called Māheśvara; but it is doubtful whether the term was used on Wema's coins in the strict sectarian sense as suggested by RGB.[^297_262] A coin of the Kuṣāṇa king Huvishka (106–138 A.D.) is said to have a motif similar to that of the Mohenjodaro seal of Paśu-pati.[^297_263] The end of the Kuṣāṇa power synchronised with the rise of the Bhāraśivas in northern India (cir. 150 A.D.). As [[P298]] their name clearly indicates, the Bhāraśivas were staunch followers of the Śiva-religion. So were the Vākāṭakas who were matrimonially related to the Bhāraśivas, Gautamīputra, the son of the Vākāṭaka sovereign Pravarasena I, having been married to a daughter of the Bhāraśiva king Bhavanāga.[^298_264] The period of about two hundred years from 150 A.D., which was dominated by these two dynasties, saw a kind of upsurge of the Śiva-religion. The Guptas, who came to real power in the first decades of the fourth century A.D., exhibited a remarkable catholic spirit in the matter of religion. Epigraphic and numismatic evidence indicates that most of the Gupta sovereigns were devout Vaiṣṇavas or Bhāgavatas,[^298_265] but there is also evidence to show that they and their ministers respected and patronised Śaivism without any inhibition.[^298_266] The prevalence of Śaivism in northern India in the post-Gupta period may very well be inferred from the statements of Hieun-Tsang (second decade of the seventh century) referred to by RGB.[^298_267] The Chinese pilgrim, who mentions the Pāśupatas as many as twelve times, says that there were temples of Maheśvara at which the Pāśupatas worshipped; in one or two of them they even resided. And, at Varanasi, Hieun-Tsang found about ten thousand sectaries who honoured Maheśvara, besmeared their bodies with ashes, went naked, and tied their hair in knots. It may be added that, when Hieun-Tsang travelled in India, Buddhism was rapidly disappearing from the south, while Śaivism, and especially Jainism, were becoming the popular faiths in that region.

## South Indian Śaiva Bhakti

It has already been seen that south India was the provenance and the stronghold of a prominent aspect of the proto-Indian Śiva-religion. The various other original popular religious cults of south India also showed many traits similar to those of [[P299]] the early Śiva-religion. Indeed, they all seem to have formed a more or less integrated religious complex under the supreme godhead of Śiva. It was, therefore, no wonder that Śaivism, in its new sophisticated form, found in south India a most suitable soil for a rapid and exuberant growth. What the religious situation in south India had been when Śaivism made its re-entry in its ancient home is not clear. Buddhism is known to have become firmly established in the Deccan by the third century B.C. It continued to flourish during the Sātavāhana period (cir. 200 B.C.–250 A.D.), as is evidenced by the various monuments, big and small, which are scattered in the valley of the Kṛṣṇā and its environs. There are also indications that Buddhism, Jainism, and Brahmanic Hinduism co-existed in the south during the early centuries of the Christian era. Soon, however, Buddhism gradually disappeared from the religious scene in that region, and, as elsewhere so too here, it was Śaivism which profitted most by the expulsion of Buddhism. Śaivism had thus to contend mainly with Jainism.

Śaivism in south India developed in two directions—āgamic and of Bhakti, primarily in the latter direction. And this brings us to the three stalwarts of this Bhakti movement, the famous Tēvāram Trio. According to the generally accepted tradition, the first two of the Tēvāram Trio, namely, Tirunāvukkarasar (Appar) and Tiruñānasambandar were contemporaneous (Appar being the older of the two) and lived during the reign of Pallava king Mahendravarman (610–630 A.D.), while the third, namely, Sundarar, lived during the reign of Pallava king Narasiṃhavarman II Rājasiṃha (690–728 A.D.). Though born in an orthodox Śaiva Vellāla family, Appar was attracted to Jainism in his early life and joined the monastery at Pāṭaliputra (near Cuddalore in South Arcot) as a monk. Later, when he was stricken by some abdominal disease which the Jaina Munis failed to cure, Appar is said to have become, through the intervention of his sister, the recipient of the grace of Śiva which brought him immediate relief. A staunch missionary in the service of Śiva thereby came into being in the person of Appar. Appar soon succeeded in [[P300]] converting Mahendravarman (who had been a confirmed Jaina) to Śaivism. He thus initiated, as it were, a glorious epoch for the Śiva-religion in south India—an epoch which lasted for nearly the next four centuries through the regimes of the Pallavas, the Pāṇḍyas, and the Coḷas.[^300_268] Most of the religious activity was centred round the many Śiva temples, big and small, and "the ninth century saw the establishment of the first monasteries which were to find the Śaivite regions of the South particularly favourable to their development."[^300_269]

[^300_268]: Recently, B. G. L. Swamy ("Date of the Tēvāram Trio", *BITCM*, Jan.–June 1975, pp. 119–179) has stoutly opposed the generally accepted contemporaneity of the Tēvāram Trio with the Pallava monarchs. According to him, they lived through the regimes (871–985 A.D.) of the three successive Coḷa kings, Āditya I, Parāntaka, and Uttama.

## Spread of Śaivism outside India

### Java and Bali

A brief reference may be made in this very context to the expansion of Śaivism outside India. It is well known that the first centuries A.D. saw brisk maritime and commercial activity on the part of the Indians, mostly from southern and south-eastern India, and in some cases also from Gujarat, in South-East Asia and particularly in the Indonesian Archipelago (Dvīpāntara), which resulted in the foundation of Hindu or Hinduistic kingdoms there. Inscriptions from that region, dating from the 5th century A.D., many of which employ a script similar to the one used in Coromandel under the Pallavas, show that Sanskrit was used for official and religious purposes. They also reflect the deep penetration in that region of Hindu religion and Dharma-śāstra. However, the few sources that have become available for the first two or three centuries, that is from the 5th to the 7th century, relate mainly to the powerful Sumatran kingdom Śrīvijaya which was an important centre of Buddhism. It is only the inscriptions belonging to the eighth, and more particularly to the ninth, century that bring to light the Śiva-dominated religious scene in Java.[^300_270] Scholars have [[P301]] suggested that, though in that period the princes might have been Buddhist, the people at large were predominantly Śaiva. But the fact seems to be that, in this middle-Javanese period, Śaivism not only co-existed with Buddhism of the Mahā-yāna type, but the two actually coalesced with each other.[^301_271] Beginning from 930 A.D., there were produced in East Java several Sanskrit texts with Old-Javanese paraphrases which afford glimpses into the Javanese Śaivism. Among such works may be mentioned the *Bhuvana-kośa*, the *Bṛhaspati-tattva*, and the *Gaṇa-pati-tattva*. These works seek to bring about the unification of various Śaiva sects by emphasizing their common basic teachings and practices. The *Vrati-śāsana*, a tract of unknown origin, throws ample light on the Śaiva asceticism as practised in the Indonesian Archipelago.[^301_272] From the theological-philosophical point of view, the vast body of speculative literature principally in Old-Javanese represents a stage earlier than that of the *Śaiva-siddhānta* of south India.[^301_273]

[^300_270]: It may be noted that, from the point of view of duration in the Indonesian Archipelago, Śaivism is more important; but it is the name of Viṣṇu which occurs earlier in a West-Javanese inscription of the fifth century A.D. Incidentally, the inscription of Caṅgal (732 A.D.) mentions the establishment of a *liṅga* on the Wukir hillock.

[^301_271]: This is quite understandable in view of the common source of Śaivism and Buddhism mentioned above. The situation in Java was more or less similar to that in medieval Bengal. Cf. J. Gonda, "Śiva in Indonesien", *WZKSA* 14, pp. 1–31. Śaivism and Buddhism were regarded as two brothers—the former being older and the latter more perfect. The "coalition" of the two was said to be the result of Javanisation. Also see: Gonda, "The presence of Hinduism in Indonesia: Aspects and problems", *Vivekananda Comm. Vol.* (1970), pp. 535–554. It may be incidentally added that the Hari-Hara concept also is not uncommonly represented in South-East Asia.

[^301_272]: In this work, Śiva is referred to also as Rudra and Bhāskara.

[^301_273]: Cf. Renou, *Op. cit.*, p. 98.

In Bali too, Śaivism and Buddhism seem to have been mutually complementary, but the figure of Śiva was certainly ever dominant. He was also called Rudra, Īśvara, Giripati, Pañca-mukha, and Mahā-deva; and the *liṅga-pūjā* was, of course, a common religious practice. The so-called Balinese Veda, written in a mixture of Old Javanese, Balinese, and Sanskrit loan-words, actually relates, in a large measure, to the Tantric ritual of Śaivism. The supreme god of the Balinese has been Śiva-sūrya or Śivāditya with whom are connected the complicated rites of Sūrya-sevana. Attention may be drawn, in this context, to the [[P302]] fact that Śiva and Sūrya are identified with—indeed, are merged into—each other even in some early Purāṇas.

In connection with the Śaivism in the Indonesian Archipelago, a special mention needs to be made of the concept of Bhaṭṭāra Guru. The concept is evidently the result of the tendency to glorify and deify the Guru or the preceptor. In the Śaiva tradition, the Guru is believed to be infused with the spirit of Śiva—indeed, he is Śiva himself.[^302_274] Agastya also plays a significant role in the Indonesian Śaivism. He is mentioned in the oldest East-Javanese tradition (760 A.D.), and the work entitled *Agastyaparva* (11th century) refers to Bhakti for Śiva.

[^302_274]: In Indonesian texts, Bhaṭṭāra Guru is actually called Jagannātha, Parameśvara, Umāpati, etc.

### Mainland Southeast Asia and Borneo

In Cambodia, the medieval princes seem to have been devout worshippers of Śiva. For instance, the Han Chey inscription of Bhavavarman, who conquered Fu-nan (6th century A.D.), opens with a *maṅgala* dedicated to Śiva. Similarly, Jayavarman II of the early 9th century A.D., is said to have introduced a new type of Tantric ritual for the worship of Deva-rāja (represented by a Śiva-*liṅga*) who came to be recognised as the tutelary deity of Kambuja from that time onwards.[^302_275]

[^302_275]: See: B. R. Chatterji, "Indian cultural heritage in Cambodia", *Vivekananda Comm. Vol.*, pp. 493–498.

In Campā, king Bhadravarman is described in the inscription of the early fifth century A.D. as having dedicated a temple to Śiva at Mi-son under the name Bhadreśvarasvāmin. There is sufficient evidence to show that Śiva was worshipped in Campā both in the anthropomorphic and the *liṅga* forms. Indeed, most of the kings of that country are known to have occupied themselves with the installation and consecration of Śiva-*liṅgas*. Reference may also be made here to the Wat Phu hill or the Liṅgaparvata in Laos where a temple of Śiva had been constructed. In Malaysia, on the right bank of the river Bujang, there existed a Pallava-type Śaiva shrine belonging to the sixth or the seventh century A.D. Inscriptions on *yūpas* of the late fourth century [[P303]] A.D., discovered at Kutei and Muara-Kaman in Borneo (Barhiṇa-dvīpa of the *Vāyu-Purāṇa*?) speak of the prevalence of Śaivism under Mūlavarman, the son of Aśvavarman.

### Central Asia

Śaivism seems to have penetrated into Central Asia much earlier. The Greek author Stobaeus quotes a passage from Bardasanes about the visit of an Indian to Syria at the time of Antoninus of Emesa (218–222 A.D.). This passage contains a striking reference to Ardha-nārīśvara. A mention may also be made of the so-called Tri-mūrti image with Śiva as the central figure (cir. 3rd century A.D.) from Akhun Dheri near Charsadda. But more ample evidence is available from the 7th and the 8th centuries. There is, for instance, the collection of Śaiva antiquities (7th–8th centuries A.D.) derived from Tagao and Gardez in Afghanistan. The figure represented on a wall-painting at Piandjikent in Tadjikistan, belonging to about the same period, is believed to be that of Śiva. Similarly, certain finds in Dandan-Uliq and neighbouring sites of Taklamakan desert in Eastern Turkestan have brought to light traces of the Śiva-cult and the Śakti-cult.[^303_276]

[^303_276]: See: P. Banerjee, "Hindu deities in Central Asia", *Vivekananda Comm. Vol.*, pp. 281–288.

## Śaiva sects and schools

RGB's discussion of the names and doctrines of the different Śaiva sects,[^303_277] often supported by epigraphic and numismatic evidence, is quite enlightening. However, a few more points on that subject may be added here. The Vāyu-saṃhitā of the *Śiva-Purāṇa* mentions[^303_278] four sects of the Śaivas or the Māheśvaras, namely, the Siddhāntins, the Pāśupatas, the Mahā-vrata-dharas, and the Kāpālikas. Guṇaratna, in his commentary on the *Ṣaḍ-darśana-samuccaya* of Haribhadra, also refers to the four sects, but he substitutes the Kāla-mukhas for the Kāpālikas of the Vāyu-saṃhitā. He also mentions various subsects such as Bharaṭa, Bhakta, Laiṅgika, and Tāpasa. Guṇaratna further makes the interesting observation that the Naiyāyikas are Śaivas while the Vaiśeṣikas are Pāśupatas. The name Mahā-vrata-dhara [[P304]] given by these two sources, though epigraphically attested as Mahā-vratin, does not seem to have been common, for, other names are suggested in its place, such as Kāruṇika-Siddhāntin by Vācaspati in the *Bhāmatī*, Kāṭhaka-Siddhāntin by Bhāskara in his commentary on the *Brahma-sūtras*, Kālāmukha by Yāmunācārya in his *Āgama-prāmāṇya*, and Kāla-vadana (or Kāla-damana?) in the *Vāmana-Purāṇa*.

[^303_277]: *Op. cit.*, pp. 169 ff.

[^303_278]: Vāyusaṃ. of *Śiva-Pur.* VII. 2. 31. 173.

The Vāyu-saṃhitā speaks of two classes of the Śaiva Āgamas: (1) *śrauta* or Vedic which are of innumerable kinds and (2) *svatantra* or non-Vedic (Siddhānta) which are of 10 or 18 kinds and are believed to have been composed by Śiva himself. The Śaiva Āgamas mention twenty-eight Yogācāryas of Śaivism, from Śveta to Lakulīśa, while Guṇaratna enumerates eighteen Ācāryas from Lakulīśa to Vidyā-guru.[^304_278a]

[^304_278a]: For details, see: Vrajavallabha Dviveda, '*Śiva-purāṇīyaṃ darśanam*,' *Pur.* 7 (1).

### Pāśupatas, Kāpālikas, and Kālāmukhas

Lakulīśa, who, according to RGB, was a direct pupil of Śrīkaṇṭha (mentioned in the *Mahā-bhārata*) and a contemporary of Patañjali,[^304_279] is believed to have composed the *Pāśupata-sūtras* (consisting of five *adhyāyas*), and the seventeenth Ācārya, Rāśīkara, to have written a commentary on that work known as the *Pañcādhyāyī-bhāṣya* or the *Pañcārtha-bhāṣya*. The Pāśupata worship consisted of some abnormal practices: *hasita-gīta-nṛtta-ḍuṃḍuṃkāra-namaskāra-japa-upahāreṇa upatiṣṭhet*.[^304_280] The Lakulīśa-cult spread widely and quickly—Lakulīśa himself having come to be regarded as representing the emanated anthropomorphised concept of the potent cosmic role of Śiva[^304_281] and several Lakulīśa temples having been constructed.

[^304_279]: D. R. Bhandarkar gives Lakulīśa's date as the 2nd century A.D.

[^304_280]: *Pāśupata-sūtra* I. 8.

[^304_281]: Cf. K. V. S. Rajan, *VIJ* 2, 115 ff. In the early centuries of the Christian era, there were in Western and Central India two principal Śaiva sects—the Lakulīśa Pāśupata and the Liṅga-dhārin (perhaps referring to the Bhāraśivas).

The Kāpālikas are also known as Soma-siddhāntins (presumably because they specially worshipped Śiva together with Umā) [[P305]] or Śiva-śāsanas and are, according to the *Kūrma-Purāṇa*, *veda-bāhya*, though Rāmānuja mentions *yajñopavīta* as one of their six distinguishing marks. They seem to have been first mentioned in the *Maitrī-Upaniṣad* as persons who are not worthy of association because of their revolting practices.[^305_282] Yet, this sect had an extensive vogue, as is indicated by archaeological and literary evidence. The *Śāṃkara-dig-vijaya* mentions Ujjain as the stronghold of the Kāpālikas.[^305_283] The Kālāmukha sect, which is closely connected with the Kāpālika sect, is believed to have been founded by Kauruṣya and is generally regarded as Vāmācāra Pāśupata. It seems to have migrated from Kashmir to Karṇāṭaka where it wielded considerable influence from the 9th to the 13th century A.D.

### Siddhānta-mārga and Kashmir Śaivism

The Śiva-religion sponsored by the Purāṇas is usually known as the Siddhānta-mārga. Its principal tenet is Bhakti for Śiva, and its ritual practices also are fairly sober. The followers of this school are the ordinary Śaivas. But the name Siddhānta is more commonly given to the Āgamic Śaiva doctrine of south India and sometimes also to what may be called the Vedāntic Śaiva doctrine of Kashmir.

There is some uncertainty about the schools of Śaivism in Kashmir and their literature. RGB says that "the Kashmir Śaivism has two branches, the Spanda-śāstra and the Pratyabhijñā-śāstra."[^305_284] The authorship of the first is attributed to Vasugupta and his pupil Kallaṭa, while the founder of the Pratyabhijñā school is said to be Somānanda, the author of the *Śiva-dṛṣṭi*.[^305_285] However, in the introduction to his edition of the *Śiva-dṛṣṭi*,[^305_286] Madhusudan [[P306]] Kaul Sastri characterises Somānanda as the follower of the Tryambaka school of the Kashmir Śaivism. It is pointed out that Śiva himself, in the form of Śrīkaṇṭha, imparted this doctrine to Durvāsas who, in his turn, passed it on to Tryambakāditya. It was Tryambakāditya, who, in course of time, settled in Kashmir and formalised these teachings, which later came to be known after him. Somānanda, the author of the *Śiva-dṛṣṭi*, was the twentieth in the line of teachers from Tryambakāditya. It is further suggested that Kashmir Śaivism comprises three schools — Tryambaka (which is very much older than the other two), Spanda (which is derived from the *Spanda-sūtra* and the *Spanda-kārikā*), and Pratyabhijñā which is taught in the *Śiva-sūtras* — and is, therefore, called Trika.[^306_287] Incidentally, RGB does not mention the *Spanda-sūtra* at all; there is also the tradition that the names *Spanda-sūtra* and *Spanda-kārikā* denote one and the same work; but Kṣemarāja (11th century A.D.), in his *Śiva-sūtra-vimarśinī*, specifically mentions both the *Sūtra* and the *Kārikā*. With Utpala (author of the *Īśvara-pratyabhijñā-kārikā*, the *Īśvara-siddhi*, etc.), Lakṣaṇagupta, and Abhinavagupta (author of the *Pratyabhijñā-vimarśinī*, the *Tantrāloka* epitomising the 64 non-dualistic Tantras, the *Paramārtha-sāra*, etc.), Kṣemarāja, who was a pupil and cousin of Abhinavagupta and who wrote, besides the *Śiva-sūtra-vimarśinī*, works like the *Pratyabhijñā-hṛdaya*, the *Spanda-nirṇaya*, and the *Uddyotas*, belonged to a distinguished line of teachers of the Kashmir Śaivism.

[^305_284]: *Op. cit.*, p. 183.

[^305_285]: *Op. cit.*, pp. 183; 186. Mukundaram Sastri, in his edition of the *Parā-triṃśikā* (KSS, 1918), also describes Somānanda as the founder of the Pratyabhijñā-śāstra.

[^305_286]: Pub. in KSS, 1934.

[^306_287]: The name Trika is variously explained: trinity of Pati, Pāśa, and Paśu; of Śiva, Śakti, and Aṇu (the three *unmeṣas* of the *Śiva-sūtra*); of *parā*, *aparā*, and *parāt-parā*; of Siddha, Nāmaka, and Mālinī (the three out of 92 Āgamas); etc.

### Tamil Śaiva Siddhānta

It has already been pointed out that Śaivism of south India developed in two main directions — of Bhakti and of Siddhānta. The Śaiva Siddhānta of the South arose independently and may be said to have supplemented the devotional effusions of the Śaiva saints with doctrinal reinforcement. It is, for instance, said that in the *Tiruvāśagam* of Māṇikka Vāśagar (10th–11th century A.D.), which together with the *Tevāram* of the Trio [[P307]] mentioned above, is regarded as constituting the Tamil Veda, the doctrines of the Śaiva Siddhānta have fused into the passionate experience in the heart of a Śiva-bhakta. Actually, however, in that poem the element of Bhakti is far more dominating than that of Siddhānta. "Scarcely ever", writes POPE about the *Tiruvāśagam*,[^307_288] "has the longing of the human soul for purity and peace and divine fellowship found worthier expression." The Śaiva Siddhānta was originally expounded in the eighteen Āgamas to which were added, in course of time, the ten Yāmalas and a number of Upāgamas. This doctrine was later systematized by Maykandar Karulturai (13th century A.D.) in his *Śiva-jñāna-bodham* and by his pupil Arulananti Śivācārya in his *Śiva-jñāna-siddhiyār*. Maykandar, who is glorified as the father of Tamil Śaivism, is said to have belonged to the Golaki-Lakṣādhyāyi school of Śaivism. This school seems to have been an offshoot of the Mattamayūra sect, which was originally patronised by the Kalacūri-Cedi princes of Central India and which entered into the Tamil country in the 10th century A.D.

[^307_288]: *Transl. of the Tiruvāśagam*, p. xxxiv.

### Vīraśaivas and Liṅgāyatas

Besides the Sanskrit Śaiva Siddhānta and the Tamil Śaivism there was yet another school of Āgamic Śaivism in the South, namely, that of the Vīra-śaivas or the Liṅgāyatas. Actually, the word *liṅgāyata* does not occur in any extant traditional Sanskrit work on the Vīra-śaiva religion except the *Vīra-śaivācāra-pradīpikā* where it occurs as *liṅgāyita*. The attempt to explain grammatically the word *liṅgāyata* as a Sanskrit word is not convincing.[^307_289] It is more likely that the word is a Kannaḍa *tadbhava* form of Sanskrit *liṅgavantaḥ*.[^307_290] The term Vīra-śaiva is more extensive and covers both the Ārādhyas (who continue to adhere to some Brahmanic *saṃskāras* and practices) and the Liṅgāyatas (who represent a kind of revolt against the Brahmanic orthodoxy).[^307_291] To the basic [[P308]] Vīra-śaiva doctrines of *śakti-viśiṣṭādvaita* (which is generally derived from the Kashmir *śivādvaita*),[^308_292] *ṣaṭ-sthala* (six steps to *mukti*),[^308_293] and *aṣṭāvaraṇa* (eightfold protective shield against the evils of *māyā*), the Liṅgāyatas may be said to have added their specific practices of *pañcācāra* (fivefold code of life) and *tri-ṣaṣṭi-śīla* (sixty-three rules of daily conduct).[^308_294] A mention may be made at this stage of the Śivānubhava-maṇṭapa – the religious assembly which is said to have been organized by Basava[^308_295] and to have been presided over by Allama Prabhu. It is believed that Vīraśaivism, particularly its *ṣaṭ-sthala* doctrine, received its final form through the discussions of the Śaraṇas in this gathering. It was again the Śivānubhava-maṇṭapa which proved to be the fountain-head of the remarkable Vacana literature.

[^307_291]: MACNICOL (*Op. cit.*, p. 177) observes that the religious revolt of the Liṅgāyatas was less pure probably in the motives that inspired it and certainly less worthy in its results.

[^308_292]: In a Kannaḍa work, which is significantly called *Corabasava-purāṇa*, Basava is said to have stolen, as it were, some Kashmir Pandits who later supplied the metaphysical basis for his religion.

## Distinctive features of Śaivism

Attention may now be drawn to some distinctive features of Śaivism. It may be recalled that Vāsudevism-Kṛṣṇaism originated from and centred round a human hero who came to be deified in course of time. One of the purposes of the Avatāra-theory seems to have been to provide a kind of rationalisation – and also substantiation – for such godship of a human being. Śiva, on the other hand, has been conceived as a god from the very beginning. And moreover he is conceived essentially as an earthly god – as a god who, being ever present, is intrinsically interested and involved in the affairs of man. Naturally, therefore, Śaivism does not countenance any Avatāras of Śiva. The tradition that, in each Kalpa, Śiva appears along with his four disciples is obviously [[P309]] sectarian and conventional. Actually, Śiva is believed to operate either directly or through the medium of the Guru. The peculiar concept of the Guru, which, incidentally, is not of Vedic origin,[^309_296] constitutes one of the important characteristics of the Śaiva religious ideology. In a sense, the Guru-tradition is to Śaivism what the Avatāra-theory is to Vaiṣṇavism.[^309_297]

[^309_296]: The word *guru* (in the sense of 'preceptor'), which is so common in later literature, does not occur in the early Veda. Perhaps the earliest suggestion of this concept is to be found in the reference to the Ācārya-Brahma-cārin relationship in the *Brahma-cāri-Sūkta* (*AV* XI. 5).

[^309_297]: One may recall, in this context, the Bhaṭṭāra Guru of the Indonesian Śaivism mentioned above.

### Naṭarāja and the erotic ascetic

Paradox, it is rightly observed, is the very heart of the Śaiva mythology. For instance, Śiva is often represented both as creator and destroyer. He is even said to be that power in the universe which causelessly destroys and causelessly creates.[^309_298] But there is nothing inherently paradoxical in this. The creator is also the destroyer, neither in anger nor by caprice but by the very nature of his activity.[^309_299] This ambivalence of Śiva's nature is best reflected in his dance which typifies the eternal rhythm and polarity which are at the root of the infinite creative process.[^309_300] Through the various movements in the course of his dance, Śiva brings into being the corresponding cosmic forces—his dance is, indeed, "a piece of animated symbolism."[^309_301] Śiva, the Naṭa-rāja, through the sheer rapture of exuberant power, dances the creation into existence,[^309_301a] but, through his frenzied *tāṇḍava*, he also destroys what he has created, in order that he may re-create it and thereby perpetuate the rhythm of the creative process.

[^309_298]: Cf. MACNICOL, *Op. cit.*, p. 161.

[^309_299]: Cf. ELIOT as quoted by BOUQUET, *Hinduism*, p. 94.

[^309_300]: Cf. V. S. AGRAWALA, "The meaning of Mahā-deva," *Pur.* 7 (2).

[^309_301]: RENOU, *Op. cit.*, p. 65.

[^309_301a]: Cf. ZAEHNER, *Hinduism*, p. 85.

But the apparent paradox in Śiva's personality and character is far more emphatically expressed in his portrayal as an ithyphallic Yogin. Śiva's sexuality, it needs to be noted, has mystical rather than libidinous implications. It is the manifestation of an [[P310]] upsurge of a kind of cosmic energy and life-force. Through his asceticism, Śiva is enabled to keep that creative energy or life-force under control. Śiva personifies the ideal which rejects both the licentious indulgence and the stifling continence and which promotes the controlled release of power. Tapas and Kāma may be diametrically opposed, but they are by no means mutually exclusive.[^310_302] Indeed, both are regarded as being of the nature of fire or heat. These two polar opposites, namely, asceticism and sexuality, are believed to lead to the same condition, namely freedom from contingency. It is wrong to bifurcate the personality of Śiva and argue that eroticism belongs to the Śiva of the Tantras and asceticism to the Brahmanic Śiva. This apparent *coincidentia oppositorum* – this concept of the erotic ascetic – is to be understood as the expression of the essential polarity of one single integrated whole. Śiva comprehends within himself, superbly balanced, the supreme aloofness and contemplativeness of Tapas as well as the hectic fervour and involvement of Kāma. Indeed, all opposites are beautifully reconciled in his personality and character.[^310_303]

[^310_302]: See: W. D. O'FLAHERTY, "Asceticism and sexuality in Śiva mythology", *Hist. Rel.* 8 (4), 330–337; 9 (1), 1–41; "Symbolism of the third eye of Śiva", *Pur.* 11 (2). Pārvatī is said to have loved Śiva *not in spite of* his ascetic accoutrements and third eye, *but because of them*. They made him unique; in Śiva's ascetic powers there is implicit great erotic power.

[^310_303]: Kālidāsa has given a fine poetic expression to this feature of Śiva's character in the Nāndī verse of the *Mālavikāgnimitra*.

The inadequate understanding and appreciation of the essentially integrated character of Śiva and the consequent emphasis on one or another aspect of his superficially multiple nature have given rise to an intriguing variety of Śaiva sects—the Kāpālikas with their bloody and revolting *aghora* practices, the Vāmācāra-Tāntrikas with their orgiastic rites; the ecstatic Pāśupatas; the mystic Śaiva-Siddhāntins of Kashmir; the aggressive Vīra-śaivas; and the saintly Śaiva Bhaktas of south India about whose fine devotional poems it is said that in them is to be found the frankest and the profoundest delineation of the individual religious life with its struggles and dejections, its hopes and fears, its [[P311]] confidence and its triumph.[^311_304] Such evocation of heterogeneous responses by a single godhead constitutes yet another noteworthy feature of Śaivism.

[^311_304]: Cf. ELIOT, *Hinduism and Buddhism*, Vol. II, p. 217. It is certainly not correct to say, as MACNICOL (*Op. cit.*, p. 160) has done, that Śiva is the god least likely to attract a theistic devotion. Indeed, the south Indian Śaivism gave Bhakti to the north and borrowed philosophy from it.

### Śakti and Ardhanārīśvara

It is perhaps on account of its erotic mysticism that we see in Śaivism a comparatively heavy preponderance of the female divinity. As indicated above, tendencies are evident even in the proto-Indian Śiva-religion towards the worship of the male and the female aspects of divinity in a joint divine sovereignty. In classical Śaivism, Śakti, the metaphysically derived cosmic potence of Śiva, was granted an independent status and a separate personality. Indeed, in the cosmogony of the Tantras, the role of the creator actually belongs to her—Śiva being represented as a mere immobile spectator.[^311_305] But, here too, in conformity with the usual tendency of Śaivism, the two poles, namely, the male and the female aspects of divinity, are re-united, through a balanced integration, to form the magnificent godhead of Ardha-nārīśvara.[^311_306] Kālidāsa has very poetically brought out the paradox of Śiva, who, being ever united with his consort, still is the foremost among the all-abjuring ascetics.[^311_307] It is further paradoxical, in a sense, that the lord of the Yogins and the ascetics should have been regarded as the originator and the [[P312]] presiding deity of drama and dance.[^312_308] But, obviously, this idea has its origin in the cosmic dance of Naṭa-rāja Śiva. Mention may be incidentally made in this very context of the tradition that the first fourteen sūtras of Pāṇini's grammar, which form the basis of a part of his terminology, were revealed to him by Śiva himself.[^312_309]

[^311_305]: This is reminiscent of the Prakṛti-Puruṣa relationship of the Sāṃkhya. Similar tendency is discernible also in respect of Lakṣmī, Sarasvatī, Rādhā – but only rarely and certainly not in such an emphatic manner.

### Social outlook and Vaiṣṇavism

Having originated as a religion of the people (rather than of hieratic circles) Śaivism has been more or less non-conformist in character in the sense that it generally did not make much of the traditional Varṇāśrama-dharma. It also promoted equality of sex. Some of the Śaiva sects, like the Liṅgāyatas, even went to the extent of denouncing *veda-prāmāṇya*, Brahmanic orthodoxy, image-worship, and the doctrine of transmigration. They removed the bar to widow-remarriage and strongly protested against child-marriage.

There is no doubt that Śaivism is more ancient than Vaiṣṇavism, but the latter is said to be more expansive, more attractive, and more favourable to community of worship and religious sentiment.[^312_310] Both Śaivism and Vaiṣṇavism sponsor a personal monotheism (as against the earlier pantheism), but ritual practices are perhaps more in evidence in Śaivism than in Vaiṣṇavism. While Śaivism regards the universe as the source of pain and misery – as the *pāśa* of the *paśu* – and, therefore, as worthy of being repudiated, Vaiṣṇavism looks upon it as an evidence of the lord's omnipotence and, therefore, as worthy of being cherished. But it may not be valid to assert that, on account of this attitude of Vaiṣṇavism towards the universe, its contributions to arts and literature have been richer and more profuse than those [[P313]] of Śaivism.[^313_311] Śaivism is known to have dominated the religious scene till the 11th century A.D.; but thereafter Vaiṣṇavism, which had been infused with new life in respect of both philosophy and devotionalism by the Ācāryas beginning with Rāmānuja, started to look up. And, though, today, Śaivism may reckon more sanctuaries, it is Vaiṣṇavism which is generally in ascendancy. Whatever that may be, perhaps the greatest glory of the Śiva-religion is that it can justifiably boast of the longest continuous and living tradition among the religions of the civilised world.
